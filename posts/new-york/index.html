New York

New York, New York September, 2023 Magical as Ever New York Haven’t been to NYC in far too long! In with both feet and all hands on deck in our first 24 hours. Cocktails, music, nature and lots of walking. We discovered Loulou’s up the street from our hotel in Chelsea. A fabulous restaurant with the most inventive cocktail glasses. Adam is trying to figure out how they get the drink in the globe. Classic New York scene, hanging out on the fire escape. From the Lenape tribe’s seasonal hunting and fishing camp when America was first colonized, to Pier 54, and now Little Island, this spot on the Hudson River seems to have always been a gathering place. Pier 54 was the British Cunard-White Star line’s port of departure and return for trans-Atlantic voyages between 1910 and 1935 and it famously received the survivors of the Titanic in 1912. After falling into disuse in the 70s and 80s, the area was revived by the LGBTQ community and became a part of annual pride festivities and summer events. Hurricane Sandy put an end to all that in 2012, damaging this and other piers in the area. Nine years later, Little Island has injected new life into this place steeped in history as a fabulous new community park with performance spaces, ever-changing landscaping and stunning views. What happened to all the traffic in Manhattan?! Maybe it’s the exorbitant parking rates! Hanging out at Dior. The requisite purchase of the Sunday New York Times. Thankfully we found a newsstand that actually sold newspapers! If Adam is in NYC on a Sunday, he MUST sit in Central Park and read the New York Times by the lake. While sitting here with an accordionist two benches over, a group of about 50 young women congregated behind us for a group photo. While that briefly shifted the quiet vibe, it was nothing in comparison to the sudden eruption of applause around us; a successful marriage proposal! We were there for hours as the Sunday Times is a big newspaper. This guy was playing before we got there and played on after we left. Strolling through Central Park’s many trails and pathways. I almost forget that I’m in the middle of the largest city in the US. There are the distant sounds of traffic and live music, a multitude of languages spoken around us and the rustling sounds of birds and squirrels in the leaves beneath the trees. And then, amidst this wildlife, rats! Rats channeling their inner squirrels, boldly hanging out with their species cousins, no scurrying and hiding in corners. It’s really just a matter of the right outfit and some positive PR If you miss your morning coffee, there’s always this! Testimonial Tenement Museum This is one of my favourite museums in New York. Like reading the New York Times on a Sunday in Central Park, a visit to this museum is also a must do. The tenements were home to successive waves of immigrants over time; German and Eastern European, Irish, Italian, Puerto Rican and Chinese.Between 1864 and 1935, 7,000 people lived in the 20 apartments at the 97 Orchard St tenement. And with 25 tenements per block, this area became the most densely populated single square mile in the world with up to half a million inhabitants in its heyday. Our most wonderful guide took us on a brief journey in the life of Jenny and Harris Levin who came to New York in 1899 fleeing Progroms in what is now Poland. Harris managed to save $50 (about five months of rent) to buy a sewing machine and started a garment factory in the family’s living room. The five kids who slept on mats on the floor had to be up and out early to make room for the business to churn out dresses for department stores. Harris created 130 dresses per month and, after paying his four workers (baster, sewer, finisher, presser), made $0.15 per dress and cleared $10 per month after rent and other expenses. Jenny handled the family budget, using that $10 to feed and clothe their family of seven. There was no running water or electricity in the building so Jenny would have to go down to the courtyard to draw water from a single pump in the courtyard. That’s also where the four privies were for 120 people to share, as well as the one coal storage space. Lining up for water, coal and to use the toilet is where women built community, where they were mentored by those who came before them, where they learned to manage life in this new country. Hinde Amhanitski published the first Yiddish cookbook in the US, an invaluable resource for women trying to figure out how to cook and bake without familiar ingredients. She promised her readers that if they followed her recipes, their children would not suffer stomach aches and other food related illnesses. Being observant Jews, they kept kosher. And although kosher beef was twice as expensive as non-kosher beef (12 cents/pound vs 6 cents) they didn’t begrudge the price because they had no choice. Keeping kosher was their way of life. But in 1902, the American Beef Trust which held a monopoly on kosher beef, raised the prices by 50% overnight, to 18 cents/pound. Don’t mess with women trying to feed their families! Utah

Utah, USA September, 2023 Zenning in Zion Utah is home to what it lovingly calls The Mighty Five; five of the most iconic National Parks in the US. Zion Canyon at nearly a kilometre high and 24 km long, sits hardly unassumingly in southern Utah’s Zion National Park. Mormon pioneers arrived in the 1850s and were so taken by the place that they named it after the Old Testament’s name for Jerusalem and set up shop farming. How they could focus on their animals and crops amidst this beauty, I can’t imagine. In 1904 at the St Louis World’s Fair, American artist and writer Frederick Dellenbaugh, showed his paintings of the previous year he spent in Zion. Americans had never seen such a landscape and didn’t believe it was real. The US President established it as Mukuntuweap National Monument in 1909, honouring the Paiute name for the area. In 1918, the government changed its name to Zion because it was easier to spell and pronounce and a year later it was designated a national park.A hundred years ago, the government struggled to attract visitors to this area and now the struggle is how to manage the throngs of people like us who descend upon the delicate landscape. Zion is a victim of its own success. Lying within the natural marvel that is Zion National Park is the engineering marvel of the 1.7km Zion-Mount Carmel Tunnel. Three years in the making, the tunnel pushed engineering boundaries by starting the drilling in the middle. It was built in four sections and when they joined them up, the length of the tunnel connections varied by only two inches. Pretty amazing for 1927 technology! We started out on our first hike of the day on a trail heading up to Scout Lookout. We were awestruck by the colours, magnitude and beauty of this sandstone landscape. But oh, just wait…it gets more stunning with every turn! And how about my outer layer colour choice! The hiking path snakes above the Virgin River, ultimately taking us to Scout Lookout. The Virgin River seems small compared to others but it punches well above its weight. Over the centuries, it has sliced through seven layers of sedimentary rock including 2000 feet of solid sandstone. While water insidiously seeps through cracks in our homes’ building envelopes and wreaks havoc, here it creates unparalleled beauty. A million tons of sediment is carried by this river every year, usually through flash floods. As we hiked into upper elevations, we wondered who dumped so much sand up there; sand so fine and soft it rivalled the most beautiful beaches. That would be our hard-working Virgin River! Here, it’s all about water! Navajo sandstone towers around us with the colour variation coming from the amount and type of iron oxide. As snow melt, water, ice and wind carve through the stone, curves and shapes form and one day will fall. This one above Alex looks pretty solid! Monolith after monolith after monolith… Time in this place has afforded me a whole new appreciation of rocks! This crosshatch formation shows two different types of weathering over time. The horizontal lines are courtesy of millennia of winds sandblasting the rock to expose the original sedimentary dune structure beneath. The vertical lines come from the fissures created by water seeping into the rock which are then cracked open by the constant tug of war of expansion under the heat of the day and contraction as the area cools overnight. Taking in the view of Angels Landing and confident that I don’t want to climb it! The infamous Angels Landing, named so by Ethelbert Bingham in 1916 when he exclaimed to his friends, “only an angel could land on that!” Certainly not for the faint of heart, this is considered to be one of the most dangerous hikes in the US. A thousand feet above the valley floor and no guard rails…surprising that there have only been 13 deaths in the last 20 years. And not surprising that the unofficial motto of the park is Your Safety is Your Responsibility. Navigating the Narrows The Narrows is one of Zion National Park’s most iconic hikes. This is the narrowest section of the Zion Canyon, with sandstone walls 1,500 feet tall towering over the Virgin River as narrow as 20 feet wide in some sections. The spectacularly changing landscape and colours shifting with the sun was remarkable and a real testament to Mother Nature at her finest! All suited up and ready to go. This is serious business!
