Prince Edward Island, Canada

Prince Edward Island, Canada June, 2023 Picturesque PEI Technically and locally, we’re ON Prince Edward Island. We had a four province day to get to PEI; a Toronto to Halifax flight and a drive through New Brunswick over the Confederation Bridge to PEI. This tiniest province of Canada (population of 180,000) packs a geographic punch. Never-ending beaches, not-anymore-towering dunes, more lighthouses per capita than anywhere else and world-class lobster rolls. What more would anyone want? We were met with rain but we came prepared! Eroding coastline along PEI’s central north shore. Hurricanes certainly don’t help maintain this fragile ecosystem. Seagulls galore, swooping in and out to feed on treasures nestled under the sand. Pounding surf that we learned follows the storms…the storm we drove through the night before! PEI is the source of 30% of the lobster consumed in Canada and 80% of our mussels. This is big business here. The quality of the seafood is unbelievable. As good as they are, we wondered why lobster, which has its roots as peasant food, is so expensive. Turns out there’s no farming of lobster to boost efficiency, it takes lobsters seven years to grow to market size and they change their location in the ocean at their own will (one year they may be 500m off shore and then next they maybe 3km off shore. They just keep the fishermen guessing! Our first lighthouse of the trip. Covehead Harbour Lighthouse. The bonus about this one was the timing coincided with lunch. We noticed a little fishing marina with a few shack down by the water. Down we went and noticed a place called Richard’s selling lobster rolls. We figured we needed to give it a try since we are in the land of lobster. So good and who knew Richard’s is one of the best places on the island for lobster rolls! …said lobster roll from Richard’s… Adam getting some directions from Charlottetown locals in front of St Dunstan’s Basilica. Bishop Maceachern’s boat. Along the lines of the Mailman’s creed—Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds—the first Bishop of Charlottetown didn’t let anything get in his way of making his pastoral visitations. The clever Bishop crafted this half boat/half sled to ensure the safety of all the valuable bits needed for the celebration of mass and other sacraments as he made his way throughout the settlements around the province. The rails on the bottom enabled him to travel through the snow and ice (look closely and you’ll see a pair of snowshoes strapped to the bow and stern of the boat) and if his horse pulling the sled should fall through the ice, his precious religious objects would stay afloat in the boat. PEI is the birthplace of Confederation and all the magic started right here at this table at the Charlottetown Conference. This first meeting in 1864 was intended to discuss the union of the three Maritime provinces. Upper Canada (Ontario) and Lower Canada (Quebec) asked to come as observers and they managed to switch the direction to set the stage for the union of upper and lower Canada, and the Maritime provinces. These men sealed the deal in 1867, of course without including representatives from more than half the population: women and First Nations peoples. Had to have some Cow’s ice cream while in PEI. The shop is housed in an historic building from 1901 whose original occupant was an apothecary. I guess one form of medicine to make you feel better is replaced by another. Excellent marketing for this popular ice cream…as for the product, it’s not worth writing home about… Classic red clay fields ready for planting, most likely potatoes. The very picturesque village of French River. Architecturally funny house perched by itself on a hill in French River. Inspired by The Flying Nun or perhaps the wimple (hat) of the nun’s habit of a bygone era? Dandelions are everywhere on PEI! Fields covered in white and yellow as far as the eyes can see are in aid of bees. The pollen in these early season “weeds” is critical for the nutrition of the bees before the rest of the spring and summer flowers bloom. I think we should elevate the humble dandelion to flower status.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/Oov5RVhW/img_5923-2.mp4Adam doing his part to help feed the bees. The Bottle Houses in Cape Egmont were a retirement passion project of Édouard Arsenault. His creative spark was ignited in 1979 when his daughter sent him a postcard of a bottle house during her travels to Vancouver Island. After a classic PEI career as a lighthouse keeper, lobster fisherman and carpenter, he started collecting bottles long before recycling was a thing. In those days, only pop and beer bottles were recycled. Édouard loaded up his truck with bottles he collected from the local dance halls, restaurants, the legion and the dump. In all, 30,000 bottles were used to build three structures over the course of three years. His first bottle structure (12,000 bottles) garnered the attention of the community who then turned him into a recycling depot, bringing him their bottles. This is his second structure, The Tavern, 8,000 bottles strong. This is the chapel, his third and final structure, complete with an altar and pews. Ten thousand bottles went into this one, mostly discarded votive holders from the local Catholic parishes. Very fitting. Overlooking the shore at Cape Egmont, where Édouard Arsenault was the last lighthouse keeper before it’s automation in 1958 after 74 years of various lighthouse keepers tending to the safety of sailors. Thunder Cove beach is a beautiful beach with an intricately carved red sandstone backdrop. The crashing waves on the northeastern shore have etched the layers of sandstone into precarious structures. Bay of Fundy, Nova Scotia

Bay of Fundy, Nova Scotia June, 2023 Flowing Bay of Fundy When we visited Cape Split on Nova Scotia’s Bay of Fundy, I wondered what all the tide fuss was about. Standing on the cliffs 60 metres (200 ft) above the Bay of Fundy, there were no massive tides to be observed. Maybe the tide was in at that time of day?  I now know that the Bay of Fundy is 275km long and shaped like a funnel. When the massive flow of water is pushed through a progressively narrower space, there’s nowhere for the water to go but higher and higher up the shores. Burntcoat Head is closer to the head of the bay where it’s much more narrow than the mouth of the bay where it flows into the Atlantic Ocean. The magic really comes from the precise length of the Bay of Fundy, which makes the seiche (the rhythmic movement of water in an enclosed space like an inlet)  frequency perfectly match the pulse from the Atlantic Ocean tides). This, coupled with the funnel shape of the bay, makes the Bay of Fundy the highest tides in the world. Adam has always wanted to see the tides at the Bay of Fundy and Burntcoat Head Is the site of the highest recorded tides in the world. Offered only seven days in the year due to the confluence of weather and tide schedules, Chef Domenic Padua’s Food Fantastique creates a truly magical dining experience on the ocean floor (yes, when the tide is out). Our lunch course, before heading down to the ocean floor, was a delicious shore boil. Local clams, mussels and lobster were spectacular. And there was a bit of added fun. In the middle of our circle of colourful Adirondack chairs were three buckets. No getting up to dispose of spent shells; we tossed them into the buckets throughout our lunch. Given the number of shells on the grass on one side of the trio of buckets, I’m not going to tell you where I was sitting… Flowerpot Island is a classic rock formation out here, shaped by tidal erosion. This island used to be attached to the mainland and its outer edge was the site of the original lighthouse build in 1858. But time and tides ultimately eroded the land bridge until the lighthouse could only be accessed by climbing up a ladder on the bank. That certainly made the job of the lighthouse keeper more difficult. The five kerosene lamps in there had to be cleaned five times a day. Dirty burning but not many choices in those days. So in 1913, they tore down the old one and built a new one on the slowly shrinking mainland. The back side (or front, depending on your perspective) of Flowerpot Island with caves and beautiful ledges carved by master sculptor Mother Nature. Standing at one of the outer edges of the beach at the lowest point of the tides that day. Today, the high tide measured 13m (43 ft) of its official maximum height of 16m (53 ft) measured right here at Burntcoat Head. For a bit of perspective, the worldwide average tidal range is 1m (3 ft). Enormous force is created every day here, equivalent to 8,000 trains or 25 million horses. Some efforts have been made to harness this green energy but so far, none have met the environmental muster. Our set up for pre-dinner happy hour and post-dinner story time. Ready for happy hour! Amazing how they can perfectly cook a steak, lobster and scallops in a makeshift kitchen on the beach! Here, Chef Domenic is giving us a description of what’s on our plates. He titled our dessert course “Where the Sun and Moon Meet”: local berries, lavender phyllo and Grand Marnier mascarpone cream. This is our intimate group of 11. This was 2023’s first dinner and, being before tourist season slams the Maritimes, we had a smaller group than the typical 20. We were among a lucky 130 people to enjoy this experience this year. After dinner we retired to the Adirondacks for storytelling with Glooscap First Nation Elder Lorraine Whitman. Holding her eagle feather, a symbol of truth, she shared the Seven Sacred Teachings, by which the Mi’kmaw have lived for thousands of years: Love is the eagle Respect is the buffalo Courage is the bear Truth is the turtle Humility is the wolf Wisdom is the beaver, and Honesty is the sabe (or Sasquatch) Lorraine is the President of the Native Women’s Association of Canada and is an advocate for the rights of Indigenous women, girls & gender-diverse people. She told us how the Mi’kmaw are a matriarchal society. As life givers, women and their wisdom are deeply respected, particularly grandmothers who hold vast experience. While decisions are made as a group, they are never made without consulting the matriarchs. Lorraine is known as Grandmother White Sea Turtle. In the Seven Sacred Teachings, the turtle is the holder of truth. It was an honour to spend the day with such an inspiring woman. The tide is starting to creep in. It typically moves an inch/minute but can get as fast as 15km/hour! Signs on the beach tell people they should be leaving three hours before high tide. JP, who spent some of their formative years in Five Islands across the bay from here, told us that when the clam diggers clear off the beach, that’s the time for visitors to leave. No clam diggers on the beach? Don’t go down. Every year, rescue services are called in to save people who stay too long on Two Hour Rock. The Rock’s claim to fame?
