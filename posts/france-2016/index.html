Paris, France

Paris, France Paris, France February 2025 Laid-back Left Bank You know you’re in France when, every night, your bedtime reminder pings and you’re in the middle of dinner! Paris is a pleasant assault on all the senses. The food! The neighbourhoods! The art! We spent the first half staying on the left bank by the Sorbonne with a hop across the Seine for our second half. Perfect strategy. Allons-y à gauche! The classic French street corner…cigarette, cellphone and un petit chien. The sailboats in the Jardin du Luxembourg are still here… …and being captained by the children! I’d never heard of the avant-garde artist Nadia Léger until we serendipitously stumbled upon an exhibition of her work! While she remained less known during her artistic career, she asserted her spirit in a lot of self-portraits. Don’t mess with this woman! I would love to call her a doyenne of the avant-garde but she never got that recognition. Talented, yes, but her position in the art world was thwarted by a few things. Her biggest coup but also her biggest stumbling block was her early association with Fernand Léger who completely overshadowed her. While they influenced each other to some degree, she never developed her own distinctive style. You could also blame sexism and her staunch communist leanings working against her but really, she was eclipsed by her teacher/lover/husband. Now, there finally seems to be a revival of interest in her, as we discovered! At 17, she saw Léger’s work in a magazine and decided to move to Paris from her native Russia. She found her way into his classes at the Atelier Moderne, first as a student and quite expeditiously as his mistress, then as his assistant and finally as his wife. After he died, she spent the rest of her life promoting Léger’s work. Maybe she struggled a bit with her identity as she signed her work in a variety of ways. Sometimes as Nadia Petrova (her father’s Russian name), sometimes as Nadia Khodossievitch (her given name), sometimes she inserted Léger’s name even before she married him. Her work does seem to echo his but she tended to make the subject of her paintings the central element where he was more about how the shapes and colours interacted. It feels like there’s more humanity in her work. They both painted series depicting the worker in the 1950s. Here, both titled Les Constructeurs, Nadia (left) and Fernand (right) are showing construction workers on the job. The art historians graciously give Nadia the edge on this one saying Fernand folllowed her palette and composition. All that art makes one hungry! We began our search for the best croque monsieur in Paris. And then there’s the hunt for pavlova excellence! We had an important clue on this one. We have the cookbook from La Meringaie and have made variations several times. We needed to come and see how it’s supposed to be! I’d say we have done a pretty good job but I need to up my meringue piping skills. Adam lived in Paris for the summer 34 years ago, learning French in the mornings, reading French poetry in the Jardin du Luxembourg in the afternoons and hanging out in jazz clubs in the evenings. The washroom walls in Caveau de la Huchette are full of graffiti as are most public toilets. But I can tell you I wouldn’t kiss any wall let alone a wall in a place like this. My mother taught me never to sit on a public toilet seat and I extrapolated from that. Paris 2025 47 Bread sold by weight. They cut what you want and you pay accordingly. Best way to get fresh bread daily! Notre Dame is open but not quite done with the post-fire renovation. I waited till midnight to book a pair of the 15,000 daily tickets that would have been sold out for two days hence if I had waited till the morning. The line for no tickets was long but it moves…I guess there’s only so much time most people spend in a church. What a sight to behold. They’ve done a fantastic and oh so tasteful job of bringing the majestic dame back from the ashes. They set an impressive objective of opening the doors to the public within five years and have done so without forgetting to acknowledge and thank the firefighters, the 340,000 donors who opened their wallets even before the flames were doused, and the 2,000 builders who meticulously cleaned or remade 2,000 statues, gargoyles and chimeras, the 2,000 oak trees, the 1,000 cubic metres of new stone added to the existing 41,000 square metres of freshly cleaned stone and the 4,000 square metres of lead roofing to make the dream a reality. So much was lost in the fire that may have been caused by an electrical spark or a stray cigarette butt dropped near the 1,000 year old wooden roof framing (who the heck smokes inside a job site…oh, right, the French), but what an opportunity to respect the old and bring in the new. From modern works facing classic paintings in the chapels like this 1972 tapestry based on Matisse’s “Polynésie, le Ciel Polynésie, La Mer” opposite this 1640 “Birth of the Virgin” by Baroque brothers Louis and Matthieu Le Nain… …to a fresh new bronze altar by Guillaume Bardet that looks like a salad bowl any of us would covet. And of course, any cathedral would be remiss without and information booth at the entrance and a gift shop at the exit! Baguette Jenga! Provence, France

Provence, France June, 2025 Pedalling in Provence Our last week required a big shift. Not only did I have to switch from my fledgling Italian to my deep down French, we donned spandex and spent the week biking through Provence. The scenery was stunning, the hills daunting, the wine fabulous, the villages charming and the company unbeatable. No easing in to riding on day 1! Bring on the elevation…at least it was spread over some distance. Our first home base was Vaison la Romaine. Still looking fresh as we get started on our first day. Vaison la Romaine is a city in two parts. Starting out as a wealthy Roman city with a theatre, baths, temples and villas, it was abandoned after the fall of the Roman Empire. The inhabitants figured without Rome’s protection, they’d be in better shape if they moved across the Ouvèze River to higher ground. This grew into the medieval hilltop village behind us, complete with all the trappings of medieval villages including a castle. This first century Roman bridge crossing the Ouvèze River is a testament to the magic of Roman architecture. In 1992, there was a devastating flood, dumping 30cm of rain in a few hours. The Ouvèze River rose more than 10m in less than an hour! People died, buildings, cars and bridges were swept away. But this 2,000 year old bridge withstood the torrent. It’s cherry season! Gilly just had to pull over (sudden roadside stops are so much easier on a bike!)…we didn’t notice the private property sign… Since 2023, French farmers protesting against financial and regulatory pressures have been turning village entry signs upside down. Despite the upside down sign, we still managed to find Mollans sur Ouvèze. Lovely riverside café for our first coffee stop. Rolling out of town. Mt Ventoux! Should we? Lunch in the colourful town of Buis les Baronnies. Bike parking! We met this farmer roadside selling cherries, strawberries, garlic, tomatoes and some syrups from the farm that’s been in his family for 90 years. Aside from a thyme syrup he had a poppy one. He was very clear to specify that they just use the petals… Our last stop, which was welcome in this heat, was a swim in the very cold Ouvèze River. We left Vaison la Romaine for our second day of riding. Apparently our elevation on day 1 wasn’t enough… Goodbye Vaison la Romaine! An unscheduled stop in the lovely hilltop village of Séguret which had its origins in the 10th century. Lots of artist’s galleries and shops here. Needless to say, the cobbles were a bit of a challenge. The French really pay attention to their outdoor planters! Bédoin, a classic jumping off point for Mt Ventoux. We decided not to do it as the temperatures were hovering around 35C. A beer garden in Bedouinc for those considering a ride up or those seeking a reward after the climb. The group surrounding us in matching jerseys was from The Netherlands. They were a small part of a group of 500 riders raising money for MS. At the table beside us in the left, some had just summitted Mont Ventoux twice and were waiting for their friends who were on their third round. Off to dinner in Mazan. We are staying in a château which was once the home of the Marquis de Sade. More Fun in the French Countryside The last three days of our bike trip and a quick twirl around Marseille on the way home marked the end of a fantastic five weeks in Italy and France. I am heading home with my mind full of language and my heart full of gratitude! Starting in Mazan, we did a stretched out climb along the Gorge de la Nesque. Mazan was home to a notorious historical figure, the Marquis de Sade. In fact, our very beautiful hotel here was his family home. Oh, the stories those walls could tell! Stunning vistas along the gorge but I dare say the protective barrier walls are a little low…https://videos.files.wordpress.com/GR3YaDGg/img_4529.movBeautiful tunnels cut through the rock along the gorge. Made it to the top! Up here, we met another group of Dutch cyclists rising money. This time, 800 who were heading to Ventoux the next day, riding to raise money for ALS. Roadside massage. This little honey shack was partway down the gorge road. The owner had 200 hives (now down to 130 as she is retiring in a year or two) that she moves around depending on which honey she is making. Among the honey she makes is true lavender honey. True lavender grows at altitudes of 800+ metres (where we were) and has a higher-end product than lavendin which is a man-made hybrid grown lower down. Lavendin is what we think of as the classic fields of lavender we see in Provence. .https://videos.files.wordpress.com/eGq0mtIN/f666001d-e4f8-4bc6-a879-55e66cd15ca8-2.movWhee! We left Mazan,and the Marquis de Sade’s dodgy history, for the beautiful hilltop village of Gordes. Thankfully the elevation wasn’t too great, given the temperature rose to 35C. Known as the Fontaine de Vaucluse, the water rises to 300 metres in the spring and for a few weeks gushes out of a cave behind us. At 12C it was a sharp contrast to the 35C air temperature and i actually found it difficult to breathe! I stand to make $5! Our last day was a big, lumpy loop out of Gordes…again in 35C! Bordeaux, France

Bordeaux, France May, 2022 Bottles and Beaches in Bordeaux Bordeaux felt a bit like Bologna. Big enough to have a lot of interest and small enough to not be overwhelming…a little cousin of Paris with its own young and vibrant panache, perhaps. This region is recognized as a world wine leader and it did not disappoint! Beautiful cobbled streets with the Garonne River running through it, Bordeaux has some impressive churches. Churched out from nearly seven weeks in Italy, we weren’t compelled to enter but we went into the Gothic and Romanesque Cathédrale St André because it was our first church in France. Much tidier graffiti here than in Naples. The beautiful old town comes to life in the evening with tables spilling into the streets. During our meal, a pair of minstrels spent 15 minutes serenading the diners up and down the block before passing their hat. The Miroir d’Eau, facing Bordeaux’s Place de la Bourse, is the largest reflecting pool in the world (3,450 square metres). Unfortunately it was a bit windy when we were there so it’s not as dramatic a reflection as when the water is perfectly flat. We used Bordeaux as a base and took a couple of day trips. First day we hopped on a train to Saint Émilion, merlot central. This stunningly beautiful area is the Bordeaux region’s the oldest active wine producing appellation, having been making wine since the first century AD. The beautiful old landscape filled with rows of vines and dotted with old stone buildings and towers. They are very serious here about wine and appellations. Wine from one appellation may be sold in another appellation’s area but may not be bottled there. So if you have vineyards across other appellation lines, you can’t make use of your bottling facilities one one location by bringing over your wine from next door for bottling. Makes Canada’s provincial border control seem like nothing. We rented bikes and pedalled to the outskirts of town to visit a few wineries perhaps a bit less under the tourist crush. We lucked out with a couple of lovely tours and some great learning about the region and rules in France. Interestingly, both tours were given by university business students, one of whom was doing an MBA in Luxury…quite a specific specialty that sadly wasn’t an option when I did my MBA. I can still ride in a straight line and one handed to boot! The vineyard at Chateau de Candale. An example of the rules preventing the passage of unbottled wine across appellations. These three vineyards have the same owner but have completely different wineries listed on their labels. Efficiency might suggest one label with three different wine names. Trent Mayers and Natalie MacLean, might you have something to say about this? Maybe this handy wheel will help me learn to articulate the flavours and aromas of wine. A mobile bottling truck at Bardet winery. This winery has been in constant operation since the mid-1700s. They are bottling their Dirty South label. This name is a nod to the regions poor wine reputation back in the 1970s. The winery rents the bottling truck for a day and a truck this size can fill 3,000 bottles per hour. Kind of like using a co-packer, this is a great way to keep costs down by not having to invest in underutilized equipment, but you can still control the process. Loading up the filled bottles to be taken away for labelling, boxing and storage or delivery. The next day, we took the train to the seaside town of Arcachon. With temperatures forecast to hit 34C, we thought a beach day on the chilly Atlantic would be a good idea. Arcachon is divided into four seasonal “cities”. After a lovely salad lunch in the Ville d’Été (we were in desperate need of greens and some quinoa after nearly seven weeks in Italy), we rented bikes to get to a beach further away from the throngs at the main beach in town. Beautiful cycling paths in Arcachon. Destination! The beach at the Dunes de Pilat. It feels like we have arrived in a land of giants. The water was cold but we were so hot! Trying to accommodate everyone, they install temporary stairs on the backside of the enormous Dunes de Pilat from May to November. We approached from the beach side and paused for a swim before scaling the sand, carving our own stairs only to have them blow away. The air temperature of 34C felt like 40 on the blazingly hot sand. That’s me, trudging along well behind Adam. I could blame it on a bum ankle but in reality it was hard work! Channeling my inner superhero in an effort to get to the top. I think I would have been well toasted if I didn’t have my “cape”. Top! The Dunes de Pilat. This is Europe’s largest sand dune, 110m high, 500m wide and 2.7km long. Massive! Dordogne, France

Dordogne, France May, 2022 Delicious Dordogne Why Dordogne? Honestly, one of my favourite guys at a wine store in Vancouver told me about it years ago. He and his wife would rent a holiday home there for month every year. I wondered how they didn’t get bored going to the same region again and again. So when we were throwing a dart at the map to decide where to go after Bordeaux, Dordogne came to mind. Now I can see why he and his wife returned year after year. Truffles, foie gras, markets, châteaux and many of the plus beaux villages de France. It’s a place to relax, to explore, to simply enjoy. Bliss, really. And the locals are absolutely lovely. On our way to our home for the next six nights, we stopped in Bergerac for lunch and poked around the old town. Lots of beautiful half-timbered houses and cobbled streets. It also has a famous namesake who, truth be told, never actually lived there (or even visited for that matter). Cyrano de Bergerac actually got his name from a fief somewhere in France called “de Bergerac” that his family had acquired. Some nose envy going on here… We arrived at our most lovely accommodation imaginable. Les Clos Lazcazes in Le Buisson-de-Cadouin. The absolute perfect place to stay to visit villages up and down the Dordogne and up and down the Vézère rivers. The pool was more than welcome given the few days of heat wave we had while we were there. We would return in the afternoon and cool off in the pool. Our first day we went to truffle territory. While the whole region is truffle territory, Périgeux is THE place to go for everything truffle. If we had been there in truffle season, we could have actually gone truffle hunting, but sadly we were there months too late (or early, depending on your point of view). The truffle museum was a great education for us, though. Now we know why some truffles are tasteless. Like with many foods, time and heat can zap flavour, freezing messes with texture and alcohol and oil will pull flavour out. So, know that a fresh truffle will only last 8 days and the best are the tuber melanosporum, harvested in winter. And never buy truffles that have been sterilized as the heat will kill its flavour. Who expected me to become a tree hugger? When it’s an oak with truffles growing on its roots, I’m more than happy to cozy up! A truffle orchard! A silkie chicken was hanging around our dinner table. Strangest chicken I’ve ever seen. Soft like a cat (and apparently likes to be cuddled…I didn’t try), with a black skin. Which you can see in its face. Does one eat them? Yes, edible but most people are turned off by its black flesh and bones! It was market day in the gorgeous medieval town of Sarlat. Weekly markets bounce around most of the towns in Dordogne with a market daily in a town that is surely not too far away. Olives anyone? Délices is an understatement In 1962, Sarlat-la-Canéda (its full name) was the trial town of a new law called the loi Malraux which was all about preserving the heritage of French towns. Sarlat was the first of many towns to receive funding to restore its old buildings. I think it must have received extra cash as the guinea pig and to make sure the law stuck because it is really stunning. It has the highest density of “Historic Monuments” and “Classified Monuments” of any town in France, and is apparently on the UNESCO World Heritage Site wait list A bit of levity overlooking the old cemetery at the Sarlat Cathedral The bullet-shaped Lanterne des morts is quite a mystery. This hollow tower in the cemetery dates back to 1180 and nobody is really sure of its intent. The general consensus is that a lamp was hoisted up into the tower at dusk to shine through the hole at the top to light the way for the souls. It is also thought to identify the location of a cemetery at night. And, another theory is that the light coming from these towers would keep Death in the graveyard and stop “him” from seeking out new victims. If Périgeux was truffle central, then Sarlat is foie gras central. There’s even a statue of geese to commemorate the ancient goose market at Place du Marché des Oies. Note the evidence of the trip to the truffle shop. We availed ourselves of some market treasures and had a picnic by the Dordogne. Our 28th anniversary was spent canoeing along the Dordogne past villages and châteaux. We managed to scout a few locations for visits over the next couple of days. Who needs a castle when you’ve got a canoe? Serenity Does it get any better than this? Stuck in the weeds We even managed to get a picture with Alex! Dinner was at the fabulous Relais & Château Le Vieux Logis. Great food and beautiful gardens. We had to start our 29th year with good coffee! Italy was amazing with coffee and meh, with pastries. France’s pastries are amazing but the coffee, meh. Until we discovered Céline and Julien, proprietors of Cafés Layac, in Vitrac, near Domme. Tucked beside a little school (lucky parents!) they roast their own beans using a wood-fired roaster. This pair know their stuff and make amazing coffee! They even offer roasting education and experiences. Six months in business so far, I wish them success! Domme sits high above the Dordogne. This Bastide town was among 700 built over 150 years between 1222 and 1372 as a means by which the French and the British to tried to lay claim to as much land as possible. Over time, each conquered the other over and over again throughout the various tussles and wars over territory usually in the name of money and religion. Domme
