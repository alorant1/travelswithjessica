Ravenna, Italy

Ravenna, Italy April, 2022 Ravishing Ravenna This little gem has among the most remarkable Byzantine mosaics imaginable. Monreale in Sicily was incredible and Ravenna is equally amazing and dare I say maybe more so. Eight UNESCO world heritage sites in a pretty small town is quite an accomplishment and these places are unbelievably well-preserved. The Battistero Neomiano, is the oldest monument in Ravenna dating back to the late 4th or or early 5th century. It gets its name from Bishop Neon who completed it at the end of the 5th century and added the incredible mosaics. It is an eight-sided building symbolizing the seven days of the week and the day of resurrection and eternal life. This ceiling mosaic shows John the Baptist baptizing Jesus in the Jordan River surrounded by the 12 apostles. Zeus gets a bit of billing here to personify the Jordan. How often is the adult Jesus depicted naked? Finely detailed mosaics cover the inside of the building, even the simple cross is covered in tiny gold tiles. This baptistery is deemed to be the finest and most complete example of early Christian baptisteries. So, not being particularly knowledgeable about the Christian religion, I was quite intrigued by this painting we found in the Church of Santa Maria Maggiore. St. Peter sneaks into visit St. Agatha in prison, disguised as an old man (those in the know would recognize him by his square beard and the keys in his left hand). He has come to heal her with ointments as her breasts had been cut off earlier in the day. How did she meet this unfortunate fate? Since she had consecrated herself to God, she rejected the advances of a Roman prefect. The prefect was so incensed that he had her imprisoned and tortured. St. Peter heals her and her breasts are restored. Nice idea but apparently, in reality, she died a few hours after this brutal torture. So here’s a cool thing in an unassuming Basilica di San Francesco. The crypt under the altar reveals an old tile floor, flooded by groundwater and populated by goldfish. I have to wonder how little goldfish got in there! Alabaster windows in many of the churches here. It turns out Dante spent the last few years of his life in Ravenna and after his death, he was honoured with a special tomb. Apparently fans flock to see his tomb. We joined the flock! And here you go. Dante’s tomb! That’s it, that’s all folks. Now you don’t need to line up to peek in the door to see it. The Mausoleo di Galla Placida is quite the wonder to behold. It shares a site with the Basilica di San Vitale and there seems to be a competition as to which is more beautiful. You can decide…Galla’s mausoleum is on a smaller scale but packs a different kind of punch. It’s a timed entry ticket and you get five minutes. There is literally an electronic timer that goes off and a guy comes in to give you the evil eye if you don’t get out. Galla was the emperor Theodosius I’s daughter and she’s not actually buried in her own mausoleum (she’s in Rome). Surprise! Not what you’d expect from such an austere exterior. Alabaster windows and mind blowing mosaics The entire room and ceiling was covered. Last of Galla’s mosaics. I have to stop somewhere… And finally the Basilica di San Vitale. Another austere building but don’t be fooled…it’s all about the interior! Considered to be the most celebrated Byzantine monument in the world. Like one of the churches I told you about in Palermo, here too we have a clash of the Byzantine mosaics from the 6th century and the 18th century frescoes. Pounds and pounds and pounds of gold adorn these mosaics. The Justinian mosaic (sorry, hard to see but it’s the panel to the left of the windows) used 36 pounds of gold. A mix of Old and New Testament stories are told in these mosaics. A bit more detail from the previous image. Jesus seated in the centre on a blue globe. And now we zoom across the ceiling to 18th century frescoes. I honestly think attending a long service in here would feel like the time flies by; there is just so much to look at. Circumnavigating the basilica is an upper ambulatory called the matrimoneum which was reserved for married women. And more alabaster windows! Mirror image marble on all the columns. Oh, the floors! And that’s a wrap with a fantastic lunch just outside San Vitale. Two glasses of wine with lunch. Dangerous precedent… Previous Next Previous PostNext Post

Bologna, Italy May, 2025 Bring on Bologna I said it the first time we visited Bologna, and it took two years to come to fruition, but I am back in Bologna to learn Italian! We had to get a Fist-Day-of-School photo! Who sets to go to a school in a historic building with statues adorning the staircase? We do! We spent three glorious weeks at Academya Lingua and I can’t wait to go back! What a way to learn! The school was amazing, the teachers were fantastic and we came away with way more language skills and confidence than we arrived with. Our studies were not just in the classroom. Not only is the oldest university in the western world here, Bologna is also home to Carpigiani Gelato University and as such is a bit of a gelato Mecca. It became evident that locals sit firmly on one of two teams: Team Cavour and Team Santo Stefano. This was clearly an invitation for further study. We decided to supplement our language studies with a PhD dissertation on the best gelato in Bologna. Gilly already did one PhD so it was an easy leap into a second one. And this time she had two collaborating researchers! Research design and method were easily established over our first tasting and then very serious data collection and analysis ensued. Sometimes we had to collect multiple samples in a day to ensure fair comparison without the risk of forgetting intricate details. Fortunately there were no ethical considerations involved in this research as we conducted the testing on ourselves. Our results are as follows: the best hazelnut was at Cremeria Cavour, the best pistacchio was at Cremeria Santo Stefano and the best unique flavour, cherry with chocolate and hazelnut pieces, was at Cremeria La Vecchia Stalla. The surprise result was that each one of these flavours is from a different cremeria and there isn’t ONE best cremeria. The conclusion is that when in Bologna, enjoying the best gelato involves a combination of consumption and exercise – one flavour at each location! Laurie is very serious about her studies! All that gelato feeds brain activity. One of the seven secrets of Bologna, The Whispering Gallery has a cool physics trick. If you stand in one corner and whisper, someone in the diagonal opposite corner can hear you clearly. When this vaulted portico was built in the 13th century it was a surprise to discover this unintended acoustic effect. It served the lepers of the time well as they could do confession and not put the priests at risk. Neptune’s Secret Finger is another of Bologna’s not so well kept secrets. At the right angle behind Neptune’s fountain in the Piazza Maggiore, Neptune’s outstretched thumb looks like… Was this a cheeky bit of Renaissance humour or a surprising find by a tourist who happened to look that way when walking past? Back during the second century BC Roman Empjre, Bologna was called Bononia and was conveniently located along the route between Rimini (on the coast) and Piacenza. As with much of that era, old roads and buldings were covered with new ones as society and cities modernized. When doing some renovations along a retail stretch in the early 2000s, the road was discovered under the site of what is now a Roche Bobois store. The city decided that if the store wanted to keep this location, it had to provide access to anyone who wanted to see it, regardless of their shopping intent or lack thereof. So here it is, the part of the ancient Roman road network that connected the empire, in the basement of Roche Bobois! The canale dele Moline is part of the old network of canals in Bologna and is at the end of our street (already sounding like Bologna is home away from home!). These were built during the 12th-16th centuries for agriculture, sanitation and to move goods, especially from the silk mills (moline means mills). In medieval times, Bologna was a major silk production hub with over 100 water mills powered by these canals. There is just a bit of the original 60km network of canals still visible. One day I walked past this one and it was completely drained! Turns out they do this occasionally for repairs, maintenance and cleaning (I’m sure they can get pretty stinky). A very apt name for a tattoo parlour. The longest portico in Bologna, and the world, is the Portico di San Luca. Built between 1674-1793 to protect religious pilgrimages to the San Luca Sanctuary, this special place is 3.8km long with 666 arches (a dodgy number if you ask me for a path leading to a Catholic basilica) and 489 steps up. No wonder it’s now used for a workout, in addition to the odd pilgrimage! The top of the Portico di San Luca overlooking Bologna. Every day, during our school’s morning coffee break, we were faced with this! Not sure what the name of this shop is but it could be Brushes R Us. Cinque Terre, Italy

Cinque Terre, Italy May, 2022 Charming Cinque Terre Rocky coastlines, colourful houses and pristine blue sea. Does it get any more beautiful than this? From the frenetic, bustling pace in Florence the expanse of nature has been a welcome embrace. We hiked between the five “official” Cinque Terre towns (plus a couple of “unofficial” destinations) along coastline trails, up and over mountain tops and through vineyards clinging to the steep, terraces slopes. Finally, I am not alone with my walking poles!! No sideways glances at the poles and ankle brace! Before being broadly discovered in the 1960s, these quiet fishing and farming villages toiled away at terracing the slopes of the mountains to grow lemons, grapes, olives, and apparently lots of basil as this region claims to have invented pesto. Genoa disagrees. We stayed in Monterossa, the first or the last village, depending on your perspective. This one is known for its big beach and, unlike most medieval villages, has a very modern symbol. At the end of the beach is this modern statue of the Giant (representing Neptune) holding up the terrace of a private villa. I guess if you have a statue this big, there are only so many locations in your home where you can install it. The terrain along the trails is so varied, from roots and rocks to narrow paths hugging the cliffs and routes brushing up against grape vines. The stunningly beautiful Vernazza, nestled on a curved outcrop reaching into the water. It’s impossible to hike these trails in the recommended time because there are so many views that just take your breath away. This is why I don’t take the selfies Goodbye Vernazza. How’s that for a picnic spot! Hilltop Corniglia in the distance, the only village not accessible by boat. As a result, this is not a fishing village but rather a farming one. We noticed lots of skinny bamboo growing all over the place. We wondered why they would let such an invasive species be so prolific. We figured it out/ they use it in the vineyards! Pretty painted door in Corniglia Oh, there’s the bar! Sitting 100m above the water, the way to access the train is down the Lardarina, a series of switchback flights of 382 steps, not what you looking forward to after a hike. There’s a cheater bus available for the young woman we saw who, on arrival, got halfway along the platform to the steps and said there was no way she was climbing those! Some of the hiking trails have been closed since 2010 (Manarola to Corniglia) and 2012 (Riomaggiore to Manarola) due to landslides. A bit mind-boggling to know that 12 years later they are still closed. With such an enormous reliance on tourism you would think that repairs would have been prioritized. Well, they were supposed to reopen in 2015 but further landslides thwarted those efforts. Centuries old terracing (some of which started in 900AD) is crumbling; interest in farming is waning, many terraces having been abandoned,so upkeep of the terraces is shrinking. This is not easy work. We saw people working along the paths breaking stone and rebuilding walls, massive bags of stone by their sides. The only way to get to these walls is hiking so it begs the question: how do these bags of stone get up there? Helicopters! The helicopters go back and forth picking up and dropping off the loads of stone. Traditionally no cement is used. It’s a masterful placement of large and small rocks that create the terraces and prevent erosion. All this is on the backs of the grape growers who are called Heroic Winemakers. And the wine is very good! We started hiking on day two from the last village, Riomaggiore, to Manarola. Since the main (and easy) oceanside route has been closed for 10 years, we hiked up and over. Goodbye Riomaggiore. The clock tower in the 13th century castle in Riomaggiore keeps accurate time! Ascent. While Cinque Terre is like doing the Grouse Grind every day, the key difference is no burger and fries at the top with a gondola ride down, but delicious lunch in the next village and aperitivo in a charming piazza at the end of the day. Hmmm. Maybe Grouse needs to work on its menu… That’s Manarola in the distance. I sent Adam on to hike the upper route from Manarola to Corniglia through Volastra as I didn’t think my ankle could handle it. I used my mermaid skills to lure a ferry to take me back to Monterossa. The golden light on Manarola at sunset And the golden light on us! We were walking down the street in Manarola looking for lunch and spotted these umbrellas up high. Let’s go there. La Regina di Manarola. It was fantastic. Great vibe, great decor, great food. You’ll notice a bit of a sunset in the background…we went back the next day for dinner! Cliff side hike to Levanto. Sheer drop below my feet. Nice view though. Adam stayed several metres away from the edge. Descent towards Levanto. Beautiful stone buildings, stone pillars and caps around the windows. Ha! It’s all fake. So many building done in trompe l’oeil. It’s like walking through a movie set. Genoa

Genoa November, 2024 Grandiose Genoa We decided to wing the last bit of our time in Italy, with no plan after Piemonte. The weather ended up being surprisingly fabulous for November so we headed to the Ligurian Coast to explore the Italian Riviera. We based ourselves in Genoa which I found to be a city of contrasts. Stunning architecture, great food, a plethora of palaces put to interesting use on one hand, and on the other, an edginess to the city, through its big port and its medieval windings alleyways. Oh, and there’s a handful of churches… A beautiful view to wake up to our first morning; the golden light brings a glowing warmth to the buildings climbing up the hills of Genoa from the sea. This is Genoa’s iconic main square, Piazza de Ferrari, centred around a huge fountain that looks a bit like a spaceship, much more modern than the neoclassical and baroque buildings that surround it. On the left is the Palazzo della Borsa, a 16th century building that became the stock exchange. Genoa has a strong financial history and is considered to be the birthplace of modern banking, dating back to the 12th century…long before the Bank of New York was even a twinkle in the eye of Wall Street! The stunning Cathedral of San Lorenzo’s striped façade reminds me of the cathedral in Sienna…just on a smaller scale. Rumour has it that relics of the holy grail are in a crypt under here. There is no proof and I guess it’s best not to disturb the crypt and enjoy with benefits of the legend. The stonework around the doors is incredible and hides a special little surprise! Just to the right of the entrance, a little dog has been carved into the marble of one of the columns. Legend has it that the dog hung around the cathedral as it was being built, spending its days with the sculptors. Near the cathedral’s completion, the dog disappeared and in its honour, the sculptors carved it into the column. It’s small and easy to miss; when you find it (because you are actively searching), it’s quite a reward! Centuries later, on February 9,1941, this church seems to have had some divine protection. During WWII, the British launched a bomb which crashed through the roof of the cathedral but didn’t explode. It’s still here (defused) as a symbol of peace. The black and white vibe continues inside the cathedral, accented with lots of gold. This is one of the big draws of the cathedral, the Chapel of St. John the Baptist. It’s sits, unassuming, off to the side with the other chapels, until some generous person drops 1€ into a slot and the lights come on. Frescoes, statues, stained glass and gold collide to make this a feast for the senses. This chapel was built to house the relics of St John the Baptist, but I thought we saw some of his parts in Turkey… I guess the First Crusaders who brought them here from the Middle East in 1098 left some behind. The relics are in an ornate silver casket and occasionally the public gets to see them. They are much more accessible than the shroud in Turin which only comes out for viewing on a decade basis. St John’s relics come out yearly on St John the Baptist Day (June 24, in case you want to book a ticket). We hiked up a hill one morning in search of a coffee with a view. We found ourselves in a residential neighbourhood with nary a coffee shop to be found. Many of the homes were stunning villas with all the features of the palaces in the historical centre. It was a bit mind-boggling to think that regular (sort of) people live there behind those intricate wrought iron gates. This motorbike was not parked by one of those villas…clearly it’s been here a while. The sign on its windshield said “this vehicle is subject to police checks” and was dated August 13, 2024. I guess that’s when the police came to check it out…seems like a lot of growth for only three months of abandonment. To all my friends who have had packages stolen from their front door, I think this shelf would be a perfect delivery spot. Imagine the bad karma for the thief who steals from under the watchful gaze of the Madonna and Child. The Basilica della Santissima Annunziata del Vastato is Mecca for Baroque art enthusiasts. Marble, gold, intricate stuccos and frescoes abound on the walls and ceilings in here, oozing with the wealth of 17th century; you just don’t know where to look first! The artwork in here is unbelievable. I discovered a few new (to me) techniques called chiaroscuro (contrast between light and shadow) and foreshortening that give figures a three-dimensional quality. We spent a lot of time looking way up from down below to try to figure it out. We thought it was plaster applied to the artwork to make objects like the foot in the mid-right section and the cloak draped over the railing to the left of the centre window look like they are actually sticking out from the wall. A lot of big Genoese baroque names contributed to the painting of the magical dome, people I’m not familiar with but if you’re an Italian Baroque fan, you’d be excited to hear the main artists were Giovanni Battista Carlone and Domenico Piola. Genoa’s historic centre is a labyrinth of narrow, winding alleyways called caruggi. They were designed to manage the amount of space between the buildings. An added benefit was providing shade during the hot, Mediterranean summers, not to mention protection from marauding pirates during Genoa’s maritime heyday. This one is not so narrow…perhaps to accommodate the extra foot traffic the local population may attract. One does wonder what this kid is doing at 10:30pm, all alone on a deserted staircase…and the parents are where? Probably starting dinner.

Liguria

Liguria November, 2024 Legendary Liguria There’s so much to explore along the southern edge of Liguria; you need to devote a bit of time to really see the beautiful coastal fishing villages and the inland towns. We kept to the coast, this time, where villages sit in bays, some with castles and fortifications above for protection.Genoa is kind of in the middle so we went west one day and east the next. It’s off season on the coast so the villages were pretty quiet on a weekday, some so quiet that most cafés, restaurants and shops were closed. Our first stop heading west was the medieval village of Noli whose 11th century fortress, perched on top of Mount Ursino, was an important defence against invaders and pirates who made the Ligurian Coast a regular pit stop. Snaking our way up the footpath past olive trees, churches and houses, I spied some ripe figs! Don’t worry, I was safe and scored two beauties! These curving, cobbled streets seem to stretch to magical places beckoning us. Thankfully these old buildings have these struts to keep them from falling in on each other. Noli, from the Castello at the top of the hill. For a small place, it had 72 towers and there are a few that remain. It must have been amazing to see 72 towers crammed into this town! Not only was Noli well-positioned to defend itself against invaders, it was also run by very strategically-minded people. It was a wealthy finance hub and in 1192 it had the foresight to buy its freedom and independence from the Marquis Henry Il Del Carretto for a mere 1720 Genoa Lira. Although relatively tiny, Noli managed to maintain its independence for centuries and even had its own fleet and currency. They deftly aligned themselves with their mightier cousin Genoa which proved to be quite a symbiotic relationship. An ally and port access in exchange for protection and preserving autonomy. An Italian version of NATO long ago. The empty beach in Finale Ligure where we stopped for lunch at the only open restaurant we could find. Lucky for us the food was fabulous! On to Cervo, my favourite of the day. And I can sure pick them as it turns out Cervo is recognized as one of “I Borghi più belli d’Italia” (The Most Beautiful Villages of Italy). Cervo’s roots are medieval and you can see it in the narrow, winding streets and houses that are still here and in use. Beautiful late afternoon view from the 17th century Church of San Giovanni Battista. It must have been hard to stay inside the church when you know there’s this view outside. Its nickname is the Church of the Corallini because it was financed by local coral fishermen. Sadly, they didn’t know the environmental damage they were doing to their coastline. A number of Cervo’s little alleys and pathways have been dressed up. This place is quite a haven for artists of all kinds who have helped to restore buildings and park their workshops here. Another disadvantage of off-season is none of those ateliers were open. The flip side of it being off-season is we had the place virtually to ourselves! You know real people live here when the laundry is hanging out the windows. The start of the sunset peeking through Cervo. Once we got as far as Cervo we were almost at the French border so we went to Nice for dinner! Our next coastal day trip was east towards Portofino. Being a weekend it was definitely busier, which gave the day a more upbeat vibe! We started with coffee in Boccadasse, about 20 minutes outside of Genoa. Apparently this is weekend strolling territory for the Genoese. The strolling was pretty much roadside so we moved on after we realized there wasn’t much more to enjoy other than this view back to the old town hugging the bay. Thinking about it now, I bet the prime strolling might well have been the other side of the bay in the old part of town. Now here’s the strolling spot! Passeggiata Anita Garibaldi runs a couple of kilometres along the edge of the cliff in Nervi. In case you are wondering, Anita was the wife of our Italian Unification hero Guiseppe. She too was a freedom fighter and I’m sure quite a powerful woman in her day. The views along the cliff side path were stunning…so was the summer weather in November! My favourite town in this direction was Camogli where we stopped for lunch. A fishing village for centuries, we had fabulous seafood by the boats… …accompanied by a glass of wine! Camogli is known for its colourful seafront buildings. This is not just to be picturesque, it actually has a practical purpose. It helped the fishermen identify their homes from the sea. I’m not sure what else was happening on the boats that made it difficult for the guys to find their way home… This young fellow is carrying on Camogli’s centuries-old fishing tradition. He pulled out these tiny fish within seconds of dropping in his line. He put them in a plastic grocery bag and went on repeat. The bag was flipping around beside him and it wasn’t because of the wind. And to finish the day, we found our way to the infamous Portofino. We had to go see what all the fuss is about. It was the end of the day and most things were closed except for the excellent gelato shop! My hazelnut gelato paired well with my pear and chocolate sorbetto. Portofino’s beautiful coastline. It’s worth the hype but I’m glad we were there at the end of the day when most people had cleared out. It almost felt like we had it to ourselves. No super-yachts in residence today. We hiked up a path from the harbour to the lighthouse in time to catch the glorious sunset before we leave Italy. Ciao for now bella Italia!
