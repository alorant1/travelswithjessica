Sydney, Australia

Sydney, Australia April, 2023 Iconic Sydney A perfect recharge from the go, go, go of Vietnam and Cambodia. For us, Sydney was about reconnecting with family and exploring the coastal walks.It was an absolute treat to see cousins, some for the very first time and others for the first time in 35 years! The bond of family runs deep. What a fantastic city this is! Very much like Vancouver but 2.5 times bigger…and with a much cooler accent. Up close to the opera house. I always wondered what it was made of…it’s 1,056,000 tiles! It took 14 years to build what is probably one of the most recognizable buildings in the world. The Danish architect (Jørn Utzon) had a vision and it took him six years to noodle the design through his brain to the final result. The architect started with elliptical and parabolic shapes that just didn’t work then he had his aha moment: spherical!There are 14 “shells” that make up the opera house and if you took the structure apart and reconstructed the puzzle of the pieces, you would get a perfect sphere. The coastal walk from Bondi Beach to Coogee Beach has the most stunning rock formations. Wind-whipped sandstone cliffs hug the coastline. Six km takes a bit longer than usual when the visuals are so spectacular! Passing Mackenzies Bay and Tamarama beach. This is not a swimming spot; the rip currents would surely ruin your day. But apparently surfers are immune (and maybe a bit crazy). All those black dots in the water are wetsuit-clad daredevils waiting to catch a wave…and presumably hoping to not crash against the rocks. Caught the start of the sunset at Bronte Beach before heading off to Coogee. Blissful landscape and hidden treasures oceanside. Today, the walk from Manly Beach to Spit Bridge. This walk took us through beaches, forests and ancient aboriginal carvings. Interestingly, the carvings are not protected. They are just cordoned off so you don’t step on them but otherwise, their sandstone surface is exposed to the elements, being worn away as time goes on. Maybe like our First Nations beliefs about totem poles..that they are meant to ultimately return to the earth from where they came. My first opera! And what a venue! We followed and followed and followed and ended up just left of centre stage! The stage ready for the second act. And a final walk from Macquarie’s Lighthouse to Watson’s Bay. The cliffs here are beautiful but dangerous. Well, there’s a few % added to every bill (even just a coffee) if it’s a weekend and a whopping 15% surcharge if it’s a holiday. Customers definitely pay for the privilege of eating out! Yulara, Australia

Yulara, Australia April, 2023 Sacred Uluru and Kata Tjuṯa I can’t begin to describe the beauty of the landscape in Yulara where we had the privilege of exploring Uluru and Kata Tjuṯa. The colours and views are in constant flux with the ever changing angles of the sun, from sunrise to sunset. This is an aboriginal sacred place and stories abound here from teaching sacred practices and life skills to the children, metered out as they mature, to reading the shapes in the rock and telling moral tales to keep everyone behaving properly! If you come here, a few pieces of advice: 1) We spent two nights and I would suggest 3 or 4. It’s hot, so ideally you go out first thing for sunrise and then hike, come back in the afternoon to rest and then go out again for sunset. 2) Invest in fly nets…the bush flies are harmless but an annoyance you can do without! The $15 (for two!) will greatly improve your experience!https://videos.files.wordpress.com/703S7DlX/img_4243.mp4The first approach is always special. Uluru is made up of sedimentary sandstone half a billion years old. It is 348m high with a 9.4 km circumference which we circumnavigated! Although aboriginal peoples have occupied this land for 30,000 years with the with the Anangu people being the ancestral owners, the European explorers rocked up in 1873 and tried to push the aboriginals out. Hmmm, sound familiar? A land claim was ultimately put forward in 1976 and it took until 1985 before title was given back to the Anangu. They call it “hand back”. The Uluru Kata Tjuṯa National Park is now on a 99 year lease to the government and is jointly managed by a board of eight Anangu (four men and four women) and four members of the Australian government. How progressive! Story time: The Mala people came upon this rock on their journey from the north and decided it was a good place to stay. They began their ceremony (inma) preparations. The men decorated and raised Ngaltawata, the ceremonial pole signalling the start of the ceremony. The women gathered and prepared food while the men hunted, made fires and fixed their weapons. In the middle of it all, a couple of Wintalka men showed up and invited the Mala to their inma. The Mala had to refuse as their ceremony had already begun and couldn’t be stopped. A classic case of bad timing. But the Wintalka didn’t quite see it that way and when they went home and told their people that they had been shut down, in anger they created an evil spirit – a huge devil-dog called Kurpany – to destroy the Mala inma. The kingfisher woman (Luunpa) came across Kurpany but her warnings to the Mala were not heeded. Kurpany showed up, killed a few of the men and the rest of the community fled south. Lessons learned? It’s important to finish what you start and that you should watch for and listen to warnings of danger. Peeking out an opening at the back of the cooking cave. You can still see the blackened ceiling from the cooking fires. The teaching cave is where Anangu elders taught boys to be men. They learned how to navigate their landscape and survive. The wall of this cave is their schoolroom blackboard. Generations of grandfathers painted pictures on the walls to teach and illustrate tracking and hunting. The boys would then be taken into the bush to put their newly learned skills to the test and to learn where the waterholes are (can you see the concentric circles in the wall art?) where to find animals and where to find materials to make tools and weapons. The boys are separated from their families during this time, which may last several years, until the boy proved not only his hunting skills, but also self reliance and discipline. Itjaritjari is a blind, marsupial mole that lives underground, rarely surfacing except after rain. They are small enough to fit in the palm of your hand and have a very practical backwards pouch (so the sand doesn’t get in while they’re digging). These are the mole’s holes! The old person’s cave was where elders too old to participate in ceremonies would rest and make sure women and children did not enter the ceremonial areas. Their children and grandchildren would bring them food to cook in the fire (see the blackened ceiling). The vegetation is amazing in this desert. I couldn’t get enough of these bare trees. Can’t tell you how hard it was to select one! Looks like a toothy grin. The teeth are actually bat droppings! There are 35 species of bat here, some so small that they can fit in a matchbox. The aboriginal peoples believe these caves that seem to be in the shape of a skull, were what attracted the Mala people when they first arrived in Uluru, and where they decided to set up camp. This period is called Dreamtime and the belief is the earth was created through their wanderlust. Scientists say it’s not so as the evidence of human occupation dates back a mere 30,000 years. Perfect camouflage! Glad we didn’t step on this tiny cutie. Buffle grass was introduced in Australia for pasture and dust control. It can withstand heavy grazing so it was thought to be a great solution to feeding the many camels that were introduced here with the early explorers in the 1870s. Unfortunately it had the negative effect of squeezing out spinafex grass which prevents erosion and provides a sticky substance for making traditional tools. It’s now a big problem as it has completely changed the ecosystem, displacing native plants and poses a fire risk…not a good thing in a desert like this! Foolaru? Nope. A termite mound! Adelaide, Australia

Adelaide, Australia April, 2023 Alluring Adelaide Amazing to meet up with the other half of my inspiration to travel to Australia. We’ve been planning to come for 16 years, ever since Maggie Measday and her family moved in for a short stay two doors down from our house in Vancouver. South Australia has everything you need! The beach, killer wine and a lovely city. The people are pretty awesome too. Our first landing point in South Australia! Maggie and Doug’s beach house in Seaford; it’s pretty sweet waking up to this every morning! Just a girl and her (friend’s) dog on the beach 🐾 And…the beach house is a short bike ride from wine country. We rode on a former railway line to McLaren Vale to do a bit of tasting. We’re in Shiraz land. Tasting at Gemtree, a fully organic and biodynamic winery. Nice wines…not sure I believe all the biodynamic stuff about packing manure into cow horns and burying them at ascending (or was it descending) moon phases, ground up quartz mixed into the soil to let the light in and so on. Would love to do a side by side and see if there’s a difference. Dinner at the Victory Hotel near the beach house. This is what happens when you love wine so much and you build a huge collection…you open a restaurant with an amazing cellar. No wine list for the reds. You just go to the cellar and pick up a bottle. I think I’d like to have this table next time… A walk down the pier at Port Noarlunga to the reef where there’s a marine reserve. Windy day so no reef snorkelling for us! D’Arenburg Winery’s The Cube. The winery has been in the family for decades and current owner Chester Osborn definitely pushed the creative boundaries when he built The Cube. Five mixed up levels of alternate realities art installations, tasting room, toilets worth a mention…a real feast for the senses. Wine’s pretty good too! Urinals that would give little boys nightmares! Me and my friend Ollie 🐾 The Victorian Mortlock Wing of the State Library of South Australia. Depending on which list you consult, it’s one of the 25 Most Beautiful Libraries of the World. An immersive commission by Japanese artist Chiharu Shiota called Absence Embodied filled a room at the Art Gallery of South Australia in Adelaide. Known for her string installations, this one is a giant web of 180km of red wool. A second cancer diagnosis and treatment inspired this piece. In her own words: “My body was in pieces, assembled together, but I did not feel whole. I wanted to scatter my body parts on the floor, the absence in me becomes embodied, but at the same time every single body part expresses much more emotion than my whole body could. By looking at a single contorted hand, fragmented arm or twisted foot you can feel an emotional state, a whole existence. While I was passed through the system, I felt absent from my existence, but in order to keep my existence I need to create art, I need to create Absence Embodied. Me, myself, my emotion, and the material are part of the ritual of creating art. It is not only an art piece but necessary for my life.” On the ground underneath the strung wool are a number of bronze casts of her own limbs and her daughter’s and husband’s hands, illustrating her connection to her family. Why not continue the theme? I got lucky and got a green ant in my drink! One of many cockatoos in Maggie’s garden. Maggie Beer is a well-known cookbook author and cooking show personality. She refers to herself as a cook, never a chef. She runs the most fantastic shop called Maggie Beer’s Farm Shop in the Barossa. Wow! The spreads, pâtés, cordials, sauces and more! We are now devoted dukkah fans and Adam intends to make it his mission to create his own line of dukkah. Maggie Beer also ran a show called The Cook and the Chef out of this very kitchen. Adam and I are still arguing about who’s the cook and who’s the chef. Seppeltsfield Winery is knows for its fortified wines…quite a selection that really pack a punch! Then we went into the Jam Factory…no jam in there. It was a fantastic artist’s cooperative. Wine tasting…Shiraz nouveau. Edwina! Hanging out in the trees in Adelaide. So we pulled up to our final cellar door (that’s what they call a tasting room here) called The Willows Vineyard. Pretty sure we came here because Maggie Measday’s beautiful granddaughter’s name is Willow. What a find! First, we encountered a fellow lining up all these now empty-of-handpicked-grapes buckets. We interrupted him as he was starting to give them a rinse at the end of a long day of picking. He was telling us about how they mostly hand pick, using a team of about 18. He pointed out the various sections of the vineyard. Lovely guy. We went to sit on the veranda of the old cottage for our tasting and he got back to work. Who would have thought we were chatting with Jack Scholtz, South Australia 2023 Winemaker of the Year! A well-deserved accolade indeed! In 1845, Jack’s great, great…grandfather Johann, a Lutheran, fled religious persecution in his native Prussia and ended up farming in the Northern Barossa Valley. His expertise as a bonesetter pushed him into that field more so than farming and the three subsequent generations followed in his footsteps, working in the hospital Johann founded in the 1850s. By 1936, Johann’s future generations planted grapes and here sits a fantastic winery. Lovely wines in a stunning setting with a fabulous backstory. Flinders Ranges

Flinders Ranges April, 2023 Formidable Flinders Ranges A road trip like no other! Maggie and Doug, our guides extraordinaire, took us on the most amazing adventure through the outback to the Flinders Ranges and Wilpena Pound. Storms, hiking, feral animals and unexpected mud baths! Lake Bumbunga, aka Pink Lake, feels a little like Death Valley in Nevada but there is actually water out there and the pink hue (from carotene produced by halo bacteria and dunalialla salina—not much else can live in this amount of salinity) gives it a more lively vibe. The lake shrinks through evaporation in the summer, appearing less pink with less water, and leaves behind these hard, crusty mounds of salt. There are salt flats upriver where they harvest salt for sale. Our road trip started with a 4WD trek through the Bridle Track, a treacherous drive in muddy conditions, and a wild ride on a dry day. Best not to have lunch before embarking on this 10km drive leading off a survey road…and best to go with Doug who takes the rocks, ruts and edges like a pro! We fit right in like locals! At the top of the Bridle Track overlook. Just need you to note how clean the truck is… The spectacular view from the top. The magic of dead trees in the landscape. The outback is a pretty arid place and only the hardiest flora and fauna survive. One of many emu we encountered. There’s really something so pretty about the emu, with its blue face and it’s natural bustle of feathers that sashays side to side as it runs. Our first hike took us to Arkaroo Rock, a significant painting site of the Adnyamathanha people who first lived on this land. The rock art at Arkaroo Rock, telling some of the stories of the creation of Ikara (aka Wilpena Pound). Ikara is a large sickle-shaped amphitheatre 17km long and 8km wide, surrounded by mountains on three sides with only a narrow gorge opening at one end. Aboriginal legend tells of Ikara being created by two giant snakes who couldn’t move after eating all the people gathered in the area for a ceremony. Queensland, Australia

Queensland, Australia April, 2023 We finished our time in Australia in the north east of the country and explored a whole different kind of nature. The Daintree Rainforest, full of plants in overdrive, and the Great Barrier Reef, in all its brilliant and fading glory, were a privilege to visit. It is not lost on us that this is a dwindling world that we humans need to work hard at keeping alive, for our planet’s sake. Dazzling and Drippy Daintree Rainforest Welcome to the Daintree Rainforest, the oldest continuous living rainforest in the world (10 million years older than the Amazon). The Kuku Yalanji tribe are native to this land and shared a spiritual connection with the rainforest until the first European settlers showed up in the late 1800s and started to clear the rainforest in search of gold, other metals, wood, minerals and agriculture. The rainforest has been protected from all this nasty business since 1988. Despite this sordid history, this place is unlike anywhere else in the world due to its very complex eco-system; 920 species of trees and 130 species of reptiles live in this 1,200 square kilometre area. It’s a bit of natural magic tucked away in the far north of Queensland. This tropical rainforest in the FNQ (new acronym for us: Far North Queensland) is definitely the land of big leaves, not to mention dangerous leaves that will cut you, poison you and generally ruin your trip. This is where there are serious consequences when people don’t obey the “do not touch” rule! Meet Scarface, the king of crocodiles in the Daintree River. Scarface is around 70 years old, is 5 metres long and weighs 500 kg. While crocs can regrow their teeth lost to crunching on bones and whatever else gets in their mouths, once there is nerve damage, tooth time is over. Scarface is now toothless but he’s still the patriarch around here and in his heyday, he could smash full grown cattle like tic tacs. We were lucky to see Scarface as he had recently returned from a 31 day absence. Locals speculated that he may have lost in a turf war with a couple of other dominant crocs trying to move in on his territory, he may have died of old age, or he may have met with human interference. He returned mid-March, unscathed. Nobody really knows where he went but maybe the old rascal has a new “friend” upriver? This divining rod seems to have worked! There’s the water! The rainforest certainly lived up to its name. Glad we had our Arc’teryx waterproof rain jackets that we picked up in a street stand in Hanoi. So much for waterproof! But my outback hat worked! How’s that for a big strangler fig! Birds eat the figs and then unwittingly drop the seeds on the tops of trees as they fly by. The seeds germinate up there on the treetops and the fig’s air roots find their way down to the forest floor and create their own root system separate from the host tree. Ultimately, the strangler fig covers the host tree with its own trunk and that’s the end of the host. The host tree dies and eventually it’s trunk rots away and the now hollow centre of the strangler fig provides refuge, feeding and shelter to a bunch of forest creatures. Cape Tribulation, the only place in the world where two World Heritage listed areas meet; the Daintree Rainforest and the Great Barrier Reef. The Cape was discovered by Captain James Cook who was the first European to land here in 1770. Landing is putting it mildly…his ship (the Endeavour) crashed into a coral while sailing through what we now know as the Great Barrier Reef. The ship was severely damaged and Cook reportedly told his crew that this is where their trials and tribulations would begin, hence the name. Mangrove forest along the edge of Cape Tribulation Beach. This area has one of the highest diversity of mangrove trees and shrubs in the world (about 30 species). These very cool trees have adapted to life in salty coastal water and have developed a salt filtration system and a method of breathing oxygen despite their roots being submerged in mud and water. An elusive Cassowary dad with a couple of chicks having a roadside drink. Despite all the rain earlier in the day, Cooper Creek didn’t overflow so we were lucky enough to get in for a swim. The Great Barrier Reef…Hanging on for dear life Ah, the Great Barrier Reef! We were fortunate to go when we did as the weather turned and trips were cancelled for the next two days. How disappointing it would have been if we had gone all that way and missed the chance to explore this natural wonder. So what’s the status of the reef? Is it dying? Is it already dead? Yes it has suffered great loss and no it is not already dead. Climate change, through water temperature and sea level changes, acidification and extreme weather events has severely threatened the reef but bad publicity is making it sound worse than it is. Cynics (like Adam) would say that’s a lot of marketing-speak to prop up tourism, and there is some of that at play for sure, I’m going to go with my new, favourite saying: the glass is half full and the other half was delicious. Some estimates show 50% of the reef is dead and climate change is making it harder for the coral to recover and regrow. We’ve all heard about coral bleaching and I learned that this doesn’t mean that the coral is dead. When the coral is stressed due to environmental changes, it expels the algae that lives inside it.
