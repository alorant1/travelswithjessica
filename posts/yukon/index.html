Whitehorse, Yukon

Whitehorse, Yukon August, 2025 Wonderful Whitehorse Why don’t more people come this magical place? My mom took me to Yukon in honour of my impending 60th, wanting to take me somewhere I hadn’t been. She succeeded with her choice of destination; it’s no longer a place I’ve never been and is a place I look forward to coming back to! Just outside of Whitehorse is Miles Canyon a key passage during the gold rush, which is what put Whitehorse on the map. Prospectors not only needed a permit to mine but were also required to carry a year’s worth of supplies. The Canadian government of the day wanted to make sure these people (many of whom were fortune-hunters and not outdoorsmen) would survive the winter. Carrying 900kg of extra supplies slowed down these hopeful folks by making them portage all their extra gear over multiple trips through the canyon. But it did give rise to a whole new portage economy! Funnily enough, these industrious service suppliers probably made more money than most of the 100,000 prospectors who set out to make it rich. Only 40,000 of them made it to Dawson City, a year-long trek, with only 3-5% making enough money to be comfortable and a mere 1% striking it rich! I guess it pays to service the hopefuls. This bridge over the canyon did not exist in 1896 when people were rushing to find gold…would have made their lives a lot easier. The water level was also a lot lower and more treacherous in the pre-dam days. The Lewes dam was built in 1957 and raised the water level nearly two feet, taming the rapids in Miles Canyon. But it messed up the salmon migration as swimming up the dam was a bit too much to ask of these senior salmon. Never fear as engineers were here to solve the problem! This is the Whitehorse Rapids Fishway (affectionately called the fish ladder), the longest wooden fishway in the world at 366m. It rises 15m, allowing the chinook to avoid meeting a certain demise in the dam’s turbines. They action only runs through here a few weeks in August so we were lucky to see the last few salmon go through. This year, 510 chinook have made it through, up from 13 in 2022 but still below the average of 860. There is also a hatchery here that helps boost the numbers that make it upstream to spawn. The post-gold rush activity saw sternwheelers making the run up and down the Yukon River between Whitehorse and Dawson City. Before the roads were built, goods and people moved on these flat-bottomed boats (shallow waters, rocks and rapids required flat-bottoms). From Whitehorse to Dawson City went everything the gold miners needed to survive: food, equipment, fuel, livestock, building materials and mail, taking a day and a half with the aid of the current. The five to six day journey against the current from Dawson City to Whitehorse brought the spoils of mining and trapping: gold, furs and passengers coming home at the end of the season. Built around 1929, the SS Klondike had a relatively short life and sank in 1936. Minus what was salvaged to build the SS Klondike 2 (seen in this picture), the OG is still submerged and very visible when the river levels are low. The larger 2.0 was completed in 1937 and doubled the capacity of its progenitor, pushing on a barge 270 tones of cargo and 75 passengers in the lap of luxury. By 1950 the Klondike Highway was built and the SS Klondike was no longer needed for cargo. They tried to turn it into a tourist attraction but that didn’t work out so well and she was retired in the mid-50s. This healing totem was raised by hand in 2012 along the bank of the Yukon River in Whitehorse. It was funded by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and dedicated to all who were affected by the residential school system; the children who were taken, the families that were gutted and all who came after that suffered the ripple effects. The pole symbolizes the reunion of separated families. Starting at the bottom are the representations of Mother holding a bentwood box, followed Children and Father, and then Wolf and Crow, the main Yukon First Nation clans, all together once again. Every woodchip was collected during the carving process to represent the people impacted by residential schools. These chips were burned in a sacred ceremony and placed in the bentwood box held by Mother, symbolizing the return of those taken. 2025 is the 10th anniversary of the Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre’s Shakaat Artist-in-Residence Program. First Nations artists from around the country are invited to apply to come to Whitehorse for 15 days. They work in small cabins by the Cultural Centre with their door open as an invitation to passers by to come in, meet the artist and learn about their work. The art of all this year’s artists will be displayed in October in the cultural centre. This year’s theme is Summer Camp. Carissa Waugh is a beader from Whitehorse. To her, summer camp reminds her of her grandmother and fishing. You can see her design plan sketched on the paper. Baby salmon are covered on top by the mother salmon in green beadwork and below are seven salmon eggs representing seven generations. When Elders speak of thinking “seven generations ahead,” they are asking us to consider the consequences of our actions roughly 150–200 years into the future. Carissa told me that her generation is the 7th since the start of the residential school system. When she finishes the beadwork, she will sew these pieces onto a piece of hide. Tara Francis is a Mi’kmaq artist from New Brunswick. Her work as an artist-in-residence this year is quilling.
