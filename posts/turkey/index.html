Istanbul

Istanbul April, 2024 Incredible Istanbul Here we are, 30 years after we switched our honeymoon destination from Turkey to Portugal after some terrorist activity made us nervous. Despite the rampant inflation (our taxi driver told us how the price of milk went up tenfold over the past three years), there’s a lot of action and a positive vibe here. Street vendors and restaurant staff stand outside their shops trying to entice us in. They are funny (“hi, can I take your money?”), persistent, but not in an annoying way and when you turn them down they laugh and say “ok, maybe later?” There are so many shops and restaurants in this city, one has to wonder how they make any money. The history is long and rich and full of intrigue. So much to share that I will divide it up into a few posts. Who knew our hotel faced the Blue Mosque?! Spectacular location! First morning, Adam dove right into a Turkish coffee. The bottom third of the cup was coffee grinds. Nobody official was around to tell his future but I could tell part of it with confidence; that there would be no more Turkish coffee in his future. He’ll stick to his regular dopio espresso! This is a real level up for topiaries. Step aside braided Benjamin Figs and make room for this Crape Myrtle. How amazing would this be in bloom! Wandering the streets outside the tourist areas takes us to the most beautiful places where real people live. Chatting with the neighbours. Anyone need an anchor? Good luck getting one out of this stack unscathed. This takes pick-up-sticks to a whole new level! It’s cherry blossom season in Istanbul too! Ah, the land of fakes. The markets are full of fake everything from shoes to handbags to jewellery to watches. The funny thing is they are all the same pieces from shop to shop…and there are hundreds of shops. So if you want Chanel shoes, or an Hermes bag, or a Rolex watch, or a Tiffany bracelet, there is actually a very limited selection. İsimlerin Şehri İstanbul (Istanbul City of Names) is a 2022 sculpture by Kurdish artist Ahmet Güneştekin at the Galataport’s waterfront. The many names of Istanbul through the ages make up this giant ring attesting to the long, vibrant and ever changing history of this magical city. Woman Life Freedom, by artist Mohammad Javad Moradi was commissioned by parsa coffee roasters and Ropi Karaköy Coffee in Istanbul in 2023. MJ Moradi is an Iranian artist who expresses his ideas through portraits with an emphasis, as you can see here, on eyes and lips. The slogan “Women, Life, Freedom” was started by the women-led Kurdish Freedom Movement in the early 2000’s in response to persecution from the governments of Iran, Iraq, Turkey and Syria. It exploded as an international slogan with the protests following the 2022 death of Masha Amini at the hands of the Iranian police for refusing to wear a hijab. Fishing off the Galata Bridge. Hundreds of fishermen (and some women) are here from morning until late at night catching buckets of sardines, bluefish, horse mackerel and Atlantic bonito. Once the city cleaned up the toxic waters of Golden Horn about 15 years ago, the fish returned and the stench disappeared making it a haven for local fishers. Traffic was insane when we arrived on the Friday after Ramadan ended. We realized it was the end of Eid and everyone was out to enjoy what they’d been missing for the past month. Traffic in the old city was at a patient standstill. No honking, no squeezing past cars (single lane after all), just waiting. And nobody seemed fussed that a taxi or a bus just stopped to wait for passengers and completely blocked everything. People on their feet are another story. Pushing and shoving to get though a tunnel lined with shopping stalls. Jam-packed with everyone swapping the crush of quiet cars on the street above for the crush of impatient people below. Grand Bazaar…on your mark, get set, go! The Grand Bazaar is the world’s oldest and largest covered market. Since 1461, shoppers and merchants have met along these halls which now are a maze of 56 interconnected passages covering nearly 56,000 square metres and housing 4,000 shops. The passages making up the Grand Bazaar started out covered overhead with tent canvas. The canvas gave way to wooden planks and now we walk beneath stone and brick vaulted ceilings. Not as crowded in the Grand Bazaar as it would be during full on tourist season but lots of shoppers and observers nonetheless. These gentlemen deliver tea all day long to the shopkeepers. They rush around the bazaar with these trays carrying tea with sugar and spoons. When empty, they return to tiny little tea service stations between the stalls where fresh tea and clean cups are at the ready. Shop owners drink tons of tea thanks to the guys that deliver it throughout the day. Empty cups and plates sit on the floor by the shops, ready for pick up. The Spice Bazaar section has the most incredible array of tastes and smells. Colourful and tasty dried fruit and nuts. Tea like I’ve never seen before with the names describing the multitude of ailments and problems they solve. Everything from diabetes, flu and coughs, belly aches, cholesterol, hypertension and gastritis to helping with love, relaxation, slimming and detox. I guess the Brits have it right, when they say all is well after a cuppa… And the spices! Can I bring this home?! I can’t leave out the Turkish Delight. This is nothing like the sickly sweet stuff we get at home. This is downright addictive. I’m not much for the jelly ones but the rolls with the creamy centres! Cappadocia

Cappadocia April, 2024 Captivating Cappadocia Cappadocia seems right out of a fairytale with its unusual landscape full of magical rock formations and unbelievable refuges. The Silk Road ran through here which brought invaders galore trying to lay claim to these lands. The ingenuity of the generations upon generations of locals is remarkable.Nowadays, it’s a tourist haven. There don’t seem to be any locals here except those working in the tourism sector. But that’s ok. It’s a classic tale of a cool place that has been discovered and shared. People make a handsome (maybe…inflation is insane here) profit from us and that’s the price we pay to experience this natural and historic wonder. We just have to look past the commercialism, which is not to hard to do in such a stunning place. Making use of every corner to sell their wares, this would be my favourite shop. Too bad my floors and surfaces are well-dressed at home. This is when Adam got in trouble. He climbed up on the photo op tractor and was summarily dismissed for standing rather than sitting. No second chances. I managed to snag the photo before the big, burly guy got close enough to be a threat. Stunning sunset our first night in Cappadocia. A short hike across town took us up to Sunset Point. In a land of valleys, there sure is a lot of climbing here! The lovely village of Göreme, set in the rock formations in Cappadocia. Embracing Turkey in all ways! Which valley? Why, Pigeon Valley, of course! Pigeons were essential to life over the millennia in these parts. Their poop was valuable as a fertilizer for crops, their eggshells were mixed with clay and water to make plaster to cover the porous walls and enable them to paint seccoes (basically dry frescoes), the egg whites were used to make paint and of course their meat served an important nutritional purpose. While dovecotes (little openings in the rocks where pigeons perched and pooped) are everywhere, this valley has the highest concentration of them, hence the name. An excellent symbiotic relationship here; pigeons get free housing and people get their poop. Not sure we can create such a symbiotic relationship at home with our volumes of pigeons and seagulls… A four-storey dwelling in Pigeon Valley, now lined with carpets and cushions to attract visitors but in the day these rooms would have housed families, with kitchens, food stores, bedrooms and maybe even livestock. Note the dovecotes dotting the walls. The pigeons were trained to recognize where they lived and always came back. Cappadocia is made up of a number of valleys full of magical rock formations. These tall, spire-like formations are geologically called hoodoos, but here they are lovingly called fairy chimneys. The story goes that when people came upon them many years ago, they couldn’t imagine they were natural and thought they must have been chimneys made by fairies who lived underground. Millions of years ago in this volcanic area, ash would rain down on the landscape following eruptions. The ash hardened into tuff, which was not so tough after all. It’s soft, porous rock that eventually was covered in much harder basalt. Add millennia of rain and wind and the softer tuff eroded into pillars more quickly than the harder basalt which capped and helped protect the softer pillars. The result is forests of pillars that look like giant asparagus or mushrooms. Devrent Valley is full of fairy chimneys that seem to spark our imagination (in English, this is often called Imagination Valley). Can you see what we see? A donkey, a camel, a regal woman, a man in a pointed hat (perhaps picking his nose) and an outstretched hand reaching for the sky. Zelve Open Air Museum deserves much more time to explore than we had. This is an astonishing Byzantine neighbourhood of fairy chimneys that were carved out and made into dwellings, churches, wineries, granaries and even a police station! Churches were the main game in town here. I wondered why they needed so many in a relatively small community but it turns out this was one of the first seminaries in Cappadocia so as a priest’s training ground I guess they needed plenty of locations for the throngs of man who heard the calling. Believe it or not, people lived in these caves until the 1950s when the government moved them out to new digs about 2km away. Running water and electricity must have been a shock! This is one of the more fun rooms. Here they made wine! The dark arch in the very back was where they dumped all the grapes for stomping. Notice the little hole to the lower left inside a smaller arch on the floor. That’s where the juice came pouring out. They would then transfer the juice to amphora to get going on the wine making. Amphora are notoriously unstable with those pesky round bottoms so these people cleverly made dents in the floor to hold them upright. I have to say this is a very dusty place so I’m pretty sure the sediment in this wine would have been akin to the grinds left behind in Adam’s Turkish coffee a few days ago! My mother dresses me… Get ready for a cat fight! Even though food is put out for the cats and there is plenty to go around, food competition is bred in the bone. A local resident was renovating and noticed that his chickens were disappearing through a hole in the wall. He pulled down the wall and found a tunnel that led down into the city. After this find, 600 more tunnels to Derinkuyu hidden in homes in the area were discovered. Ephesus

Ephesus April, 2024 Extraordinary Ephesus Ephesus is a pretty magical place. Walking the streets here takes us back 2000 years. It’s beyond impressive how advanced the inhabitants of this place were. It always blows my mind how archaeologists can stitch together history by painstakingly unearthing rocks buried metres below the ground we walk on today. Not only that, but how they can identify the different occupants and rulers of a place layered through time. The town of Selçuk, a few kilometres from the site of ancient Ephesus, is a lovely spot worthy of exploring. It’s stork season in Selçuk (suburban Ephesus, if you count the suburbs 3km away)! Hundreds of thousands of storks return to Turkey from sub-Saharan Africa in March to breed and nest. In Selçuk, there are about 375 nests. These birds build massive nests weighing anywhere from 60-250 kg and like to build them on top of anything with a view; electrical poles, tops of ancient columns or aqueducts, tops of anything high. As they build their nests and settle in, there’s a lot of poking and prodding going on and this has caused problems particularly when they choose the top of an electrical pole. Power grids have been affected and entire neighbourhoods have lost power when those long and strong beaks hit a wire. So the government has installed wide “baskets” for them to build their homes. They like being high up, not just for the view and strategic protection, but for practical reasons too. Their wing span is about two metres so taking off would be akin to paragliding; best done from a high spot. The storks stick around till the end of August and then head back to Africa. They are creatures of habit and try to return to the same place every year for breeding and nesting so every March, Turkish residents anxiously await what for them is the first sign of spring. Here’s a government-provided basket. It was either very windy (which it was) or this stork is a bit late to the game. Local wine tasting in Sirinçe. Turkey isn’t known internationally for its wines despite grapes having been grown and wine produced here for millennia…all were nice enough but nothing I would bring home. A masterpiece in Selçuk that doesn’t quite rival its famous neighbour. The Temple of Artemis is one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, partly due to its enormity (four times the size of the Parthenon in Athens)…not that you can tell from this picture of all that’s left of it. This temple, in honour of the goddess of lots of important things, notably chastity, hunting, wild animals, forests, childbirth, and fertility (not sure why she was burdened with so many portfolios), was started in 550BC and took 120 years to build. That’s how massive it was. It was put through its paces over the centuries, having been destroyed and rebuilt three times, the final straw being in 401AD after it was destroyed by a Christian mob. All that’s left is this single colum which, surprisingly, still draws in the tour busses. Note the stork’s nest sitting on top of the column. Also notice the citadel in the background. This guy in front of Ayasuluk Castle is desperate for a sale. He will sell anything…even offering up his grandmother! We heard an odd knocking sound and realized he was pounding one of the metal statues of the Virgin Mary on a big rock, trying to make it look distressed and perhaps more authentic. Selçuk’s citadel crowns the highest point in town, Ayasuluk hill with Ayasuluk castle sitting just below it. This is the citadel you can see from the Temple of Artemis. Proximity is important and I’ll get to that in a moment. The Basilica of St John is here and is the main draw. Here’s how it all came to be. When Jesus was crucified and his followers were imperilled, Jesus asked his most trusted disciple, John, to get his mom, Mary, out of town. John and Mary found their way to Ephesus and stayed together for the rest of their lives. It’s a bit unclear when Mary died but John lived to the ripe old age of 100. He left town a few times to preach and was ultimately exiled to the island of Patmos (after a few death threats), in a persecution campaign by Domitian. He came back to Selçuk in 95AD, sat on this hill and wrote his gospel and letters until his death. He is buried here according to his wishes. When Christianity picked up pace in the 3rd to 4th century AD, a Monumental Tomb was built over John’s grave. What’s left of the tomb is the square four pillared structure behind us. Then in the 6th century AD, emperor Justinian built a massive basilica around the tomb in John’s name. He collected much of the building materials from the destroyed Temple of Artemis just down the hill. Waste not, want not. A hundred or so years later Arabs started raiding the area so the citadel was built above the basilica along with walls around the temple to protect it. You may remember there were a few interesting relics in the Topkapi Palace in Istanbul, notably part of John’s arm and skull…so what’s under the tomb behind us? Was his grave ransacked? We’ve arrived at the ancient city of Ephesus! This stand was outside the gates but I wonder how brisk business would have been for him in the agora in its heyday. Would the emperor of the time have tolerated fakes, no matter how genuine they might be? The main thoroughfare, Curates St, joins the upper and lower parts of this unbelievably well-preserved city. Ephesus had a long list of characters vying to rule it over the thousand years it was inhabited. Pamukkale

Pamukkale April, 2024 Purifying Pamukkale In ancient times, Pammukale’s big draw was its hot springs and nothing has changed in the last few millennia. People still flock here to dip in the healing, mineral-rich waters full of calcium, sulfur and silica. Lots and lots of people here in the water…and of course no chlorine or other such chemicals to keep it clean. Let’s hope the natural chemicals and minerals can do that job while also healing us pilgrims of our various ailments! We visited on a super windy day… The water no longer fills all the pools. Not sure why, but it’s diverted to keep certain pools full while others are dry. Regardless, the empty pools travertine formations are as stunning, but in a different way, as the full pools. And here we go. The magical travertine pools. Overcast and windy, the colours are not as dramatic as they normally are but still a wow! Pammukale means Cotton Castle; these travertine formations look like a castle of white, floating clouds. Windy, windy! Rookie mistake…I’m dressed to blend in. So how did this beautiful terraced landscape form? Like with much of the cool geological world around us, we can thank volcanoes and earthquakes. Eruptions created terraces, earthquakes caused cracks, water seeped in and and helped carve shapes on its way downhill. A bunch of mineral-rich hot springs bubble up and when the temperature of the water drops below 35C, the calcium precipitates out and leaves these deposits in its wake. Throw in a few million years and you get this! Every morning, they refresh the waters in the pools and then divert the flow for the rest of the day. This pool is cool at this time of day but would have been warm first thing in the morning..and maybe the pristine quality of the water has been used up by the few thousand toes that have been dipped in here today… Emperor Hadrian’s letter to Hierapolis from the first century AD. I wonder with letter carrier drew the short straw and had to deliver it! Sticking with the pool theme, Cleopatra’s Ancient Pools you can actually swim in, among bits of ruins. If it’s good enough for Cleopatra, it’s good enough for me! It was around body temperature but every now and then there were warmer spots…legitimately so, I’m sure, coming from the springs below. We weren’t super impressed with the ruins of Hierapolis, especially after Ephesus, kind of like the bad strategic decision we made years ago when our kids were small and we went to Disneyland before Legoland. We almost didn’t climb the hill to enter the theatre. Thankfully we did! This is one of the most impressive theatres of all. Carved into the hills overlooking Hierapolis, I think this is the most magnificent part of this ancient city. This first century theatre was rebuilt in the second century AD by Hadrian after an earthquake and then renovated again in the third century AD under Septimus Severus. Each time, it got bigger and more impressive. By the end of the renovations, it could hold 10,000 people and is said to be one of the best preserved theatres in the world. I haven’t seen them all but I wouldn’t disagree. One of the most amazing parts of this theatre is the backdrop. Pluto’s Gate (the Gate to Hell) came about when some clever people noticed their animals were dying near a cave here. It turns out toxic gasses from a hot spring inside the cave was the culprit and the Greeks and Romans came to associate this site with the god Hades and the underworld. Antalya April, 2024 Antalya Abounds with Archaeology We feel like we’ve hit full on summer in Antalya with hot weather (38C) and blue(-ish) skies but locals look surprised when we comment on how hot it is. This is not hot, they tell us. Come back in a month to see what hot is. Capping one end of the Lycian Way (famous trekking route), this resort area is very popular with Russians. While a full 25% of tourists here hail from Russia, tens of thousands of Russians and Ukrainians have come here to avoid Putin’s war. Aspendos is another golden oldie, dating back to the 500s BC. This wealthy city of about 20,000 had a lot going for it. Lucky for them they had fertile soil and clever people which put them on a good financial footing from their olive groves, vineyards, salt beds and horse breeding. In fact, they were so known for their horses that Alexander the Great levied an annual tax of 4,000 horses on the city. What I loved most about this ancient city was its theatre (again, the theatre!), reputed to be the best preserved theatre of the ancient world. The theatre was built under the reign of Marcus Aurelius in 160-180 AD, and like most theatres of this time, the seating for the 9,000 spectators was dug out of the slope of the mountain. The coolest thing is that it is still an active theatre! Ataturk came to Aspendos in 1930s and announced that the theatre should be restored and the doors should not be locked. It should be visited and used. To this day, shows and events are held here, as you can see by the covered sound booth in the middle of the seating. I also love all the legends attached to these places. Like so many, this legend is about a beautiful young woman, in this case Belkis, the King’s daughter. The king decided that whoever created the most useful building in the city could marry his daughter. Suitors started working on their designs. Clever way to get a city built! At the end, the choice came down to the theatre vs. the aqueduct. He chose the aqueduct. Water was critical to life in the city. What the king didn’t know was that his daughter was in love with the architect of the theatre so she insisted he take one more look. When he climbed the stairs to the top of the theatre, he heard a voice saying “the king’s daughter should be mine. They made a mistake”. It was the architect whispering to himself on the stage. The king was so impressed with the acoustics, he chose the theatre! Stunning portico behind the last row of seats. Just imagine the mischief that could have taken place back here! And entertainment meant gladiator fights and the slaughter of exotic animals. Kaş May, 2024 The Kaleidoscope of Kaş Five minutes after arriving in Kaş, I was ready to settle in for a good, long stay. This is a laid back rocky beach town with a nice hippy vibe and a crazy surf when windy. Kaş sits about one third of the distance from the start of the Lycian Way, a 520 km walking route from Fethiye to Antalya. We are driving…with a bit of hiking mixed in. Our first night in Kaş. Nothing beats sunset by the water with a beer in hand! We took a day trip to this little Greek island 2km off the coast of Kaş. This cute little island now seems to have a primary purpose as a day trip destination from Kaş but in its heyday, it seems everyone wanted a piece of it. The simple folk from the Neolithic times would never have imagined the strategic interest of this 12 square km. Dominated by Rhodes, then the Romans, the Sultan of Egypt, the Ottomans, the Italians, the French and the British. It’s now a tiny Greek hotspot for people looking to get away from it all! There are around 500 people who call this home and another few thousand that show up to stay in their fancy villas every now and then. One of the big claims to fame of Kastellorizo is its Blue Cave. You have to know where to go and Nikolas kindly showed us the way. They say Finland is the happiest country (I’ve been there and that would not have been my personal assessment) but I think Nikolas is one of the happiest people I’ve met. He is a 60-year-old fisherman with two grown and successful daughters and a Finnish ex-wife (who he visits in Finland every December). Born into a longstanding family of fishermen, he has lived here all his life and says he is 100% satisfied with his life. He says if he died tomorrow, it would be fine as he’s lived a perfect life. He lives on a nice boat, has a couple of fishing boats he uses when he’s not taking people like us around. Good luck finding it on your own…this is the entrance to the Blue Cave. The entrance is a one metre opening and the tides will determine if there’s room for a boat to get in. We made some new friends and lay down in each other’s laps to clear the entrance. The Blue Cave is much larger than the Grotta Azzura on Capri, and in our opinion, much nicer on so many levels. We spent around 15 minutes in here, alone for most of it (compared to about the allocated two minutes in Grotta Azzura with dozens of other boats). We didn’t have to wait to get in and we didn’t have to pay an entrance fee. Where does the magical colour come from? When the sun is low, in the morning, the light refracts through the water outside the cave and reflects in through the narrow opening. St George Island is only accessible by boat and our lovely guide Nikolas offered to drop us off for an hour. We couldn’t imagine what we would do for an hour on this little strip of land but we seemed to find all sorts of adventures. We started by scrabbling our way in flip flops over a series of sharp, craggy boulders to get to this little beach. A swim was in order. I found out after the swim what was lurking in the water… Pufferfish abound around this island. These unassuming creatures are considered to be the second most poisonous vertebrate in the world (after the golden poison frog). This young man showed up (after we were finished swimming) with his rod and net. He had a shiny lure on the end of his line which attracted these blue eyed beauties. He lured them within reach, scooped them up with his net and then flipped them out on to the stone patio to meet their ultimate end. I think he was paid on a piecework basis for this job. He took it very seriously. Small but venomous with a vicious tooth to do its dirty work. A journey down twisting lanes always yields such beauty. Not sure which is more beautiful, the flowers and stone in this narrow lane or the sea it leads to. At a certain point in its history, it was used to incarcerate delinquent knights. Not a bad view from prison. Definitely worth the day trip! Sharing the sunset! Thanks FaceTime. Myra was one of the principal six cities in the Lycian League, a group of 23 cities that together, had the social and political strength to survive many of the invaders of their time. This is the seat of democracy, the first known democratic federation where each city in the group had a proportional number of votes based on its size, and inspired today’s democratic systems. The Persians ultimately took over, followed by Alexander the Great around 330BC (Alexander seems to have been pretty much everywhere in the 330s BC), then the Romans and then the Turks (same cast of invaders, different place). But given their strength in numbers, they didn’t succumb fully to their invaders and stayed productive and prosperous in their own right for a long time. Fethiye

Fethiye May, 2024 Fabulous Fethiye Nature continues to guide our wrap up of Southwestern Turkey, with our base at the start of the Lycian Way. Of course there are ruins around and about that deserve a bit of exploring but around here, we were mostly in canyons, atop mountains and at the beach! Saklikent Gorge is another spot reminiscent of Utah for us. Impressive at 300m deep, we trekked through rushing, icy water, without any of the fancy waders and footwear protection we had in The Narrows in Utah! Early in the day, the sun looks like fire on the rocks. Take it from us, flip flops are not recommended footwear for this type of trek. Every step sank into mud as fine as icing sugar that held on tight to our flip flops making our passage quite slow. But you wouldn’t want to do this in bare feet because within the mud are rocks you can’t see through the swirling silt. While we didn’t spread mud over our bodies, as others do here (not sure what special properties are in the mud) we did engage in a bit of graffiti. This was the end of the road for us. Just beyond the rock where Adam is standing, is a tiny but very deep pool. Glad the teenagers ahead were able to illustrate this for us. Glad we weren’t there to see this boulder fall… One of the most amazing things that can happen when travelling is crossing paths with special people in your life. My cousin Lori was trekking part of the Lycian Way and we happened to be in the same place at the same time for one day! Hardly any English is spoken in this Southeastern Turkish town, considered to be among the most devout cities in Turkey. Prophets, bazaars, music and beautiful architecture make this town a real draw for Turks of all stripes from far and wide. A multi-century debate among scholars, historians, archaeologists and whoever else thinks they know surrounds the birthplace of Abraham. For Turks, it was right here in Mevlid-i Halil cave (Birth of a Prophet Cave). The story goes that in 2000 BC, the Assyrian King Nimrod’s fortune teller warned him of a child who would destroy his religion and demolish his kingdom. With fortunes lacking a certain degree of accuracy, the king didn’t know who the pesky child would be so he ordered all children to be killed. The fortune teller, with solid accuracy, should have told him that killing all children would destroy his kingdom all the same. Anyway, a young woman called Nuna didn’t want her baby to suffer this fate and hid her pregnancy from those around her and then camped out in a cave until she had her baby Abraham. Göbeklitepe

Göbeklitepe May, 2024 Göbeklitepe: Ground Zero Enough about the ancient peoples we’ve encountered in Turkey; the Hittites, the Lycian’s, the Greeks, the Romans, the Ottomans that shaped this country and culture. Let’s go way back to where everything we didn’t know about civilization really started.Before global warming, there was an ice age…and then things warmed up about 12,000 years ago and set a new stage. Long before the pyramids and Stonehenge, well before someone invented the wheel and when the first written word wasn’t even a glimmer of an idea, Göbekli Tepe was under construction as the world’s first place of worship. Tepe means hill and as we look off into the landscape around here, there are an abundance of hills that are probably hiding more clues to the beginning of civilization as we know it. How inspiring to be standing before where it all began, the origin of the human story! Sonar surveys have exposed at least 25 sites in this area so Göblekitepe is the tip of the dawn of civilization’s iceberg. The start of civilization is defined as when society moved beyond hunter gatherers to groups of people working together to shape and control their environments and create long term settlements. To do this required organization of labour, administration, communication of shared goals, leading to complex architecture, art and social structures. We used to believe this all started around 4000 BC when our ancestors figured out agriculture and trade that allowed them to stay in one place. Nope. It was 12,000 years ago in the pre-pottery Neolithic period (or Stone Age). How do we know it was the pre-pottery era? No fragments of pottery are found anywhere around here. Good deductive reasoning. Göbekli Tepe has all these features of civilization but was pre-agricultural, turning on its head the notion that agriculture was the root of civilization. The groundbreaking (literally) notion: faith drives civilization. Hunter gatherer communities in the area built these temples at different times over 1500 years, renovating and modifying over time to suit their needs. We can see four of them here and another two behind this site are busy being excavated and are not open to the public yet. Organized workforce was required to create and erect these monoliths. The largest weighs 40 tonnes (most others weigh in at a paltry 15 tonnes). To pull this kind of project off, they needed a disciplined communal effort and a system to organize that effort. This is another huge contribution to our knowledge of the history of humanity. Before this discovery, we thought that the Neolithic people had a hard life and a simple primitive one as it pertained to art, social life and beliefs. This discovery has proven that art, social organization and beliefs were basic elements of life, as important as food and shelter. Clearly, it was important enough to be worth this massive effort! There’s no evidence that anyone actually lived here. Rather, Göbekli Tepe was a spiritual place that people came to for worship. We have to wonder how it is so well preserved for 12,000 years. The people of that time can take credit. It appears the buildings were fully cleaned after they served their purpose and then backfilled with stones, earth and debris. As a result, earthquakes, looters and others who would destroy sites like this were thwarted. This is Building D at the back of the group of four and is the best preserved. These are the largest monoliths uncovered so far. If you zoom in, you can see that the tall pillars represent people; images of arms wrap around the sides and meet in the front and they are wearing belts around their waists. Animals carvings are sprinkled throughout (not so much in this photo). In the realm of early spirituality, the pillars were likely gods and the animals were totems or protectors. People came from far and wide to worship here. They needed to be fed so maybe that precipitated agriculture? Animal reliefs are usually depicted in action with geometric patterns between them. These are complicated bits of iconography and not random bits of art. These enclosures, pillars, reliefs and statues are expressions of the beliefs and myths of the people of this period in time. This is a pretty special relief as not many are so pronounced, almost like a sculpture stuck on the face of the pillar. Another bit of animal action here with a lion or a panther ready to pounce…perhaps attacking the boar below. Here’s an interesting one. This is thought to show the idea of a sky burial, which is when a dead body is placed high up a mountain to feed animals and vultures. Here is a vulture lifting human head to the sky. And a pigeon taking in the scene to which it may aspire…human heads, watch out! The site was officially discovered in 1994 but a farmer named Şavak actually found one of the first artifacts of Göbekli Tepe, a small statue, in the mid-80s. He brought it back to the village and people were ashamed to look at it because it featured a phallus. He kept it for three years under the hay in his barn because everyone in the village made fun of him. Knowing it must be important, he gave it to the archaeology museum in Şanlıurfa who also didn’t want to pay much attention to it as it was indecent. When German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt came in 1994 and saw this “vulgar” piece in storage at the museum, he asked where it was found and booted up to the area to meet Şavak. The rest is history! Trabzon May, 2024 Tea Time in Trabzon Our time in Turkey is winding up on the Black Sea Coast. This is yet another completely different place in terms of geography, culture, food and even language as there are a whole lot of dialects spoken among handfuls of villages that are spoken nowhere else. In this region, it’s all about the tea. We were lucky enough to be here during harvest and got to see all the action related to this important crop. Turkey is among the top countries producing the best quality tea in the world, but as the world’s highest consumers of tea per capita, it doesn’t rank even in the top ten tea exporters. Clearly, Turkish tea drinkers are the priority here.  The Sümela Monastery sits high up the Karadağ Mountain. Of course there’s a magical story of this place, seemingly lodged in a cliff on a steep slope. Back in the 4th century AD, the Virgin Mary appeared in the dreams of a couple of Greek priests with the important message that she would show them to way to a cave in the Mela mountains where St Luke had painted an icon of her. They set off in their journey and lo and behold, they found the cave and the icon. What better place to build a monastery! The monastery has been renovated, expanded, looted, damaged and rebuilt a few times over its couple of millennia in existence. And here it remains, hanging onto the edge of the mountain, shrouded in mist. The chapel built over the cave. Covered in frescoes painted in three time periods starting at the beginning of the 18th century, many have been vandalized. Aside from graffiti and marking the profession of one’s love for another (does that really impress the person of desire?) many eyes and faces of these frescoes have been scratched out. Why? Could be iconoclasts who object to icons and images that don’t fit with their beliefs or perhaps looters who don’t want to be “seen” in their acts of thievery.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/x71SZN8m/img_6723.movInside the chapel, the fresco stories depicted are more about Jesus’ beginnings and life.
