Hanoi, Vietnam

Hanoi, Vietnam March, 2023 Hustling and Bustling Hanoi The capital of Vietnam moved around according to the preferences of the prevailing imperial dynasty. Vietnam’s first capital was briefly in Ninh Binh when the country gained independence after 1000 years of Chinese domination, but with a change in dynasty came a change in the capital. After spending nearly 150 years in Hue with Vietnam’s final imperial dynasty, Ho Chi Minh moved the capital back to Hanoi when he declared himself president.This place is a wonderful assault on the senses. The food is amazing, the action in the street is constant and the beeping of horns is ceaseless. Hanoi is known for its traffic. Rear view mirrors are only for decoration; the horn is the primary navigation tool. Traffic lights and which side of the road you’re supposed to be on don’t matter. Bicycles, cars, mopeds and people weave around each other, slow down and rarely stop. Drivers of vehicles 50 CCs and less don’t need a driver’s license…and it shows! As a pedestrian, you need to grab your confidence by the horns and just cross the street at an even pace. Don’t surprise the vehicles and they won’t surprise you. Absolutely everything is for sale on the sidewalks, from car and scooter parts to apparel (knock off or otherwise) to every imaginable culinary ingredient. Here, raw beef sits (ahem, unrefrigerated) beside a fellow making crab paste from live crabs. This strawberry seller, who comes to the city daily from her farm, was sold out the next time we passed her. Such a cool little cafe with great food! Hidden Gem Cafe is an eco-friendly spot with all recycled furnishings. Old seats from cars, filled tires as table tops, not to mention great murals; all sorts of weird and wonderful bits and bobs make up this stylish decor. Silly pedestrian, sidewalks are for scooters, not people. Fabulous Cha Ca for dinner. Turmeric-marinated catfish is cooked on the table with mounds of dill and green onions. Add-ins of peanuts, noodles, mixed herbs and dipping sauce make this Hanoi-born dish magical. We had no idea how to eat this but a helpful waiter came along and layered up our bowls with deliciousness! Birds and tiny catfish in cages and bowls by the Tran Quoc Pagoda on West Lake. The woman tried to sell me some fish. I wondered why…couldn’t she see that I wasn’t a local? Where would I cook such a thing? Turns out that you buy these birds and fish to release them back to the river or sky and give them their freedom. In Buddhism, we seek to do nice things for others so providing freedom to a caught creature would qualify. And being adjacent to the pagoda would mean that your act of kindness would surely be noticed. Interesting, someone catches and traps, someone else purchases and frees, and the cycle continues…with an economic side benefit! The stumpa of the Tran Quoc Pagoda certainly makes a statement. The perfect kumquat tree represents life. The fully mature orange represents the grand parent, the green orange represents the parent, the tiny green bud of an orange represents the child and the orange flower represents the future generation. This photo is of a perfect tree. Here’s a Where’s Waldo challenge for you: can you find all the generations? Creative display of intricately cut paper. Added bonus: when the police appear, the seller can fold up the umbrella and make a speedy escape. Fishing in the Red River. Squatting is such a natural position here. Mature pomelos are grafted onto a host tree for New Year. This way, the host tree continues to feed the fruit and prolong its freshness. The tree branch is sliced and the stalk holding the fruit is inserted into the cut on the main branch. It is then wrapped in tape to hold it in place. The Confucius Temple of Literature was established in 1070 by one of the earliest Kings. The goal was to educate the best and the brightest. Prospective students had to jump through many hoops to be accepted at what became the first university of Vietnam. First, students studied at the village level and had to pass special exams. They then proceeded to study for provincial exams and only when they passed the national level exam were they admitted to the Temple of Literature. Age of the student and how long it took to gain admittance was not relevant. The fact that a student passed the necessary exams was all it took. Students ranged in age from as young as 14 to as old as 60. These stone stelae, mounted on the backs of turtles (which represent longevity, strength and intelligence), were inscribed with the year of examination, the dates of the reigning kings and the names and places of birth of the graduates. Perhaps this inspired today’s publishing or the CPA graduates in the newspaper? Turtle and stork represent friendship and community. There are two stories about why this is so. 1) When there was a flood and the stork had nowhere to land, the turtle offered up his shell as a place for it to rest 2) A farmer had a pet poisonous snake. During a period where there was no food, the snake wanted to kill the farmer for food. The snake asked the turtle if this was ok. The turtle said it was ok, but crane overheard and killed the snake. The turtle’s penance was to carry the stork on his back. I like the first story of cooperation and compassion. Egg coffee, a traditional Hanoi beverage. It’s like liquid tiramisu! It was created by a bartender in 1946 who came up with the idea of whisking in egg as a substitute during a milk shortage. I’ve never seen anything like Hanoi’s train street. Sa Pa, Vietnam

Sa Pa March, 2023 Sweet Sa Pa Sapa is in the northwest corner of Vietnam. It is home to 54 ethnic minorities in Vietnam, primarily the Hmong, Red Dao, Tay, Giay and Xa Pho. The different groups are identified by their traditional clothing (colours, embroidery, head scarves, etc).At the turn of the 20th century, the French colonists realized there was substantial tourism potential here and started developing the area. Thankfully the ethnic minorities were not squeezed out and their culture and way of life is preserved. The ethnic minorities we encountered (primarily Black Hmong and Red Dao) were happy to share their culture with us. Our first hike in the mountains below the town of Sa Pa took us through beautiful terraced rice fields. Taking pictures of rice fields is like taking pictures of sunsets…the next one is always better than the previous and you end up with a ton of photos! Up here, there is only one growing season for rice (unlike in the south where there are three crops) so the rice grown here is predominantly for the families to eat themselves over the course of the year. You’d think that’s a lot of rice for the year, but these households are multigenerational. Three maybe four generations live together and each person eats rice with every meal…a lot of rice…three to five bowls of rice (per person) with each meal. Planting starts in May for an October harvest so the preparation of the fields is just beginning now. These fellas help prepare the fields by walking around the terraces, eating the weeds and fertilizing in their wake. Our first hike in the mountains below the town of Sa Pa took us through beautiful terraced rice fields. Along the way, we encountered Chi, a woman of the Black Hmong tribe with surprisingly good English. She walked with us, chatting along the way, and I thought I had made a new friend! Needless to say, when we parted ways, her true motivation came out…she unpacked her basket to show us her handicrafts…no pressure, of course. But she was my new friend, so… Typical clothing of the Black Hmong is a black base fabric with this type of embroidery. They make their own clothing from scratch, right down to weaving the cloth from hemp which they grow JUST for the stalk. Of course the leaves are discarded… Draped across Chi’s body and wrapped around her left hand is the hemp she has gathered that will be dried, boiled to soften it, twisted into long strands and woven on a loom. The resulting fabric is off white which is then dyed with various leaves and roots. As she walked along the path before we met her, she was busy hand-winding the hemp into a skein to keep it from tangling. Crossing a bridge over the river at the bottom of the valley, a bunch of little kids ran front the opposite end to greet us in the middle. How cute, I thought, coming to greet us as we approached their village. I realized naïveté caught me again when I noticed the ringleader clutching a fistful of woven bracelets. They rattled off a few English words and I realized we were as much part of their business as we were contributing to their English language education. Chi would have started out this way too, picking up language by interacting with tourists. Chi’s English was limited as she could only ask the questions she knew and respond to me if she recognized the words I was using. She had a basic banter which worked pretty well. A Hmong woman demonstrating how they weave the hemp fabric. It all starts with the bundle of hemp leaning against the loom. We are calling it the Frankentruck (in homage to Alex’s university housemate who was a builder of Frankenbikes). The take bits and pieces of vehicles, equipment and machinery and craft them into a trucks…when there is a need for hauling stuff. If there is a need for a plough, they’ll pop out the engine and craft a plough. Very efficient use of materials. There is no license plate on this vehicle because it will never go onto the official roads. It does all its travel around these mountain paths. A Red Dao grandmother heading home with her grandson on her back. A mobile gas pump. No need to leave the village to fill up. Our second day, we did a cultural discovery hike with a local Black Hmong guide. Sung is a tiny woman with an enormous smile and a wealth of information. As she shared fascinating tidbits about local plants (if you ever have a chance to chew on a cinnamon leaf, do it! Amazing spicy and sweet flavour) and her culture, we also gleaned interesting information about her own life journey. She’s the ripe old age of 26 with two kids aged 9 and 11…do the math. She was happy to share that she had a love marriage; she met her husband at a Lunar New Year party when she was 14. They married three weeks later (because it’s not acceptable to be pregnant before marriage), and she had her daughter at 15. She left her childhood home to move in with her husband and in-laws because her husband is the youngest son and it’s his responsibility to stay home and support his parents through their old age. Sung decided five years ago to be a guide so she learned English (beyond her childhood English banter she picked up selling handicrafts to people hiking through her village) and here she is today! We finished our hike with an amazing BBQ lunch prepared by a Red Dao woman. The same Red Dao woman showed us how she makes paper from bamboo at her home in the village. Ninh Bình, Vietnam

Ninh Bình, Vietnam March, 2023 Natural Ninh Bình Ninh Binh has an amazing historical legacy as the first capital of independent Vietnam and an incredible natural landscape unlike any other I’ve seen. Jungle, caves, rivers and mountains. Stunning! Temple of the Dinh Tien Hoang King. Vietnamese independence from 1000 years of Chinese domination is all thanks to Dinh Bo Linh. He pulled together an army, defeated the Chinese warlords and declared himself King in 968. Ninh Binh was the first capital of the newly independent Dao Co Viet. Things went up and down after Dinh’s assassination 12 years into his rule: sons fighting about who should be king, military strongmen trying to take advantage of the distraction, would-be conquerors trying to sneak back in. Sounds like classic royal manoeuvring and politics hasn’t changed much since the 10th century. The number of colours on the flag is meant to clear up any confusion among visitors as to whether a particular building is a pagoda (for praying) or a temple (for worshiping an important historical deities like a King or hero). This five colour flag, representing the five natural elements (wood, metal, fire, earth, water) identifies this place as a temple. Incense burning at the temple sends a message (request) to the deity you are worshiping. Vietnamese worshippers will visit different temples to improve their chances of their request or wish being granted. If the wish is of interest to the deity, chances are it will be granted so one must be careful to match wishes with the right recipient. A business request might be submitted to a successful mandarin, a family problem may be submitted to the Mother and so on. Inside Dinh’s temple with Dinh in the centre, flanked by two of his sons on the left. These two, incidentally, caused the downfall of the short-lived Dinh dynasty. Under normal royal circumstances, the eldest son would succeed the King, but Dinh decided his middle son is better suited for the job. The eldest son was jealous and had his brother poisoned. The Mandarin (the King’s right hand man) saw an opportunity to seize power and had the king and eldest son poisoned. That leaves the third son who, at the age of six, was a little underage to be King so the Queen married Dinh’s friend from his village (and commander-in-chief) Le, who rockets to the top job as King. And so begins the Pre Le dynasty. From incense in temples to fresh air on the river in Hoa Lu. Our lovely rower couldn’t speak a word of English but that didn’t stop him from telling us about various interesting things to see along the way. We climbed up to the Mua Cave lookout and I don’t want to tell you how dangerous it was to get there! View from the dragon down to the river where we just were. Lotus ponds. It’s not lotus season but imagine this full of flowers! Hiking in Cuc Phuong National Park, Vietnam’s largest at 22,200 hectares. Ha Long Bay, Vietnam

Ha Long Bay, Vietnam March, 2023 Heavenly Ha Long Bay Ha Long Bay overshadows its neighbour, Bai Tu Long Bay. And that’s probably a good thing: keeps Bai Tu Long Bay a bit off the beaten tourist path. while some 600 day and overnight tourist boats muck about and jockey for position in Ha Long Bay, a mere 35 have a license to go into Bai Tu Long Bay. Our boat company, Indochina Junk, has one of these special licenses and we were privileged to be aboard for a few nights.    As legend would have it, the gods sent a family of dragons to protect the newly established Vietnam against invaders. The dragons spit out jewels and jade which transformed into the islands and islets in Ha Long Bay, linked together to form a defensive perimeter. As invaders approached in their boats, giant rocks magically appeared just in time for the invaders to crash into them. The Dragons decided to settle in the now peaceful area. Ha Long means descending dragon (where the mother dragon settled) and Bai Tu Long means children attend upon (where the dragon children settled to attend to their mother). The geologists might disagree with how this region was formed. They dig a bit deeper back in time, starting some 500 million years ago when this area that was all part of the ocean, got hit with some tectonic movement and spent the next 100 million years forming limestone mountains. Over the last 20 million years erosion, thanks to the tides and the limestone, have created the stunning landscape of 2000 islands and who knows how many caves and water tunnels we have the privilege of navigating today. Making a cultured pearl is a little like in vitro fertilization. The mantle strip is removed from an oyster and cut into small pieces. Hue, Vietnam

Hue March 2023 Historic Hue Hue takes us back (Hue Hue back…) to the many Nguyen kings of Vietnam’s last dynasty. Hue was the capital city of this 150 year long dynasty that had an everlasting impact on Vietnamese culture history. A dragon boat along the Perfume River started our journey through the history of Hue. The name comes from the autumn drop of scented flowers from orchards upriver. In the old days, people burned incense offerings as they travelled up the river so I guess the scent was year round. The Thien Mu (Heavenly Lady) pagoda, is the symbol of Hue. Seven sacred floors each house a Buddha. Seven is a significant number in Buddhist culture for lots of reasons, among them, Buddha is said to have ascended seven steps at his birth, and there is a belief that anything more than seven days in the future is beyond human control (time to dispense with our obsession with one, three and five year plans, I guess). Another good one is there are seven Buddhist virtues: generosity, proper conduct, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, honesty, determination, good will and equanimity. I guess these didn’t apply to most emperors: those with 500 wives; those who used public funds for their own benefit; those who willingly gave up Vietnamese independence and happily operated as puppet emperors. One of 12 guards of Heavenly Lady Pagoda. Better be careful or you’ll have to contend with this guy! June 11, 1963 was one of many a day that goes down in Vietnamese history. An important image that circulated around the world that brought attention the the plight of peace-loving Buddhists. The monk, Thich Quang Duc, drove in this car to Saigon. He got out of his car, calmly sat in the middle of the street and set himself on fire in protest of the persecution of Buddhists by the South-Vietnamese government which was led by a Catholic. Despite the lack of social media in 1963, the photographs of this event circulated around the world and put pressure on the regime to address the issue. At the end of the ordeal, when his ashes were removed, they discovered that his heart did not burn. This was proof that his intentions were pure. Cynics say it was all staged and the monk was sent by the powers that be to do this to raise awareness of the issue. Noon Gate, the main entrance to the Imperial City of Hue. Well, let’s go back to the seven Buddhist virtues that don’t seem to apply to royalty… Missing from here is King #5, Duc Duc, who ruled a mere three days before he was dethroned and imprisoned. He tried to mess with the coronation protocol which involved an official statement of the incoming monarch’s positive and negative qualities. Apparently Duc Duc had some doozies in the negative column so he cheated during the coronation ceremony, asking that his negative qualities not be shared. The Queen Mother found out and out he went! We affectionately call this period “Duc Duc Go!” Hiep Hoa was King #6 who ruled a bit longer than his predecessor; a whopping four months. What did he do? Well, a bit more nefarious than Duc Duc. Hiep Hoa thought he was a puppet of his advising Mandarins and conspires to kill them off. The mandarins told the queen mother who dethroned him. He was sentenced to suicide. There is a bit of a back story here. Hiep Hoa negotiated with the French to leave the dynasty alone because his army was weaker than that of the French. He was thought to have betrayed the dynasty but in reality, perhaps he was preserving it. And so begins the era of puppet kings. Khai Dinh, King #12, the second last emperor of the Nguyen Dynasty and the second last emperor of Vietnam, was a selfish fellow. He ruled for 9 years and thought he was pretty special. He felt he deserved a pretty spectacular tomb and convinced the French to raise taxes by 30% to fund his tomb’s construction (which took 11 years). Needless to say, the people were not his greatest fans. Khai Dinh, king #12, was enamoured with western style and incorporated broken bits of European pottery into artwork. The Queen Mother Tu Minh’s rickshaw was a gift from her son, King Than Thai (king #10). Interestingly, she was also the wife of the shortest ruling king (#5)…we won’t try to navigate the provenance of the other four in between, trust me, it’s a tangled web. So, back to the rickshaw. After Than Thai was dethroned by the French (for acting against the French invaders…what’s a puppet king to do?) the beautiful, mother-of-pearl inlaid rickshaw was sold in the market to a French guy. Over a hundred years later, it reappeared in an auction in Paris. It was bought for 45,000 Euros and repatriated to Vietnam which cost another $71,000 US in shipping. If they waited a few years, maybe they could have gotten it back for free now that artifacts the world over are being returned to their original homes. Hôi An, Vietnam

Hôi An, Vietnam March 2023 Hypnotic Hôi An Loved the ancient town of Hoi An! Beautiful architecture and a step back in time. Touristy? Yes. But its charm far exceeds the tourist vibe. And then there’s there’s the countryside, which is not to be missed. The Japanese covered bridge is the symbol of Hoi An. It was built by Japanese merchants to facilitate trade with the local people living in the residential part of town. Before the level of silt rose to a degree that prevented ships coming down the river from the ocean, Hoi An was a major trading post. The construction of the bridge began in the year of the monkey (1593) so two monkey gods guard the entrance… …and the bridge was completed in the year of the dog (1595). A pair of dog gods guard the exit. The bridge has a lookout over one side and in later centuries, the Vietnamese and Chinese built a temple on the other side. The bridge remains as a symbol of cultural exchange between the Vietnamese, Japanese and Chinese people in the town. The bridge allows only foot traffic. No mopeds. No cars. If you have a bike, you need to dismount and walk across. Hoi An is subject to intense flooding. We are standing inside the historic Tan Ky house which is still in the family, seven generations later. These are water level marks showing the most notable floods. The marking out of Adam’s reach was from the biggest flood on October 7, 1964. This is a cool cup and is the only one of its kind remaining in Vietnam. If you overfill this Greedy Cup, it will magically leak. How does it work? The figure in the centre of the cup blocks the hole that makes the cup leak. If you fill the cup more than 80%, the figure floats and exposes the hole allowing the drink to leak out. Detail of one of the 100 bird panels. These panels of mother-of-pearl inlaid characters poetically describe the cold or the rain and the warmth of the moonlight. They were a gift to Le Kan Ty, the owner of the house, at his longevity wishing ceremony. Charming lantern-covered streets of Hoi An’s old town. Sadly, we can’t bring spices across the Canadian border. Those sticks would have made some killer cinnamon tea, not to mention serving as a weapon! Greens of all kinds figure very prominently in Vietnamese cooking. The very special Cao Lau noodles. These are a Hoi An speciality, made with water from a particular well. The Ba Le well, built in the 10th century, is yet to run dry. This is where the special water used to make the Cao Lau noodles comes from. A little gambling action on the market floor. This is a super complicated betting game called Bau Cua. There are six items printed on the paper: squash, crab, fish, rooster, prawn and deer. The game involves every player putting money down on a square. The winner is the one whose money is on the image shown on a six-sided die. What’s so complicated about that? Well, once the bets are placed and before the die is thrown, players can “steal” each other’s money and put it on another image. Someone has to keep track of all the movements and whose money was where before and during the shuffling because even if your money was moved by me, if the die lands on the image of where your money was before I moved it, you still win. People buy them, hang them in the temple with a wish written on a yellow card. They then light them in hopes of the wish being granted. These will burn for a month. Saigon/Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam

Saigon/Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam March, 2023 Saigon…Ho Chi Minh City…Saigon…Ho Chi Minh City… Saigon vs. Ho Chi Minh City…what is the right name? Officially, Ho Chi Minh City but locals, the Saigonese, have never accepted the name honouring the country’s first president. Most continue to refer to their city as Saigon. When Ho Chi Minh declared himself president after defeating the French colonial rule, he wanted to unite the country north to south. While the south shared that desire, they did not believe in Ho Chi Minh’s communist regime. They preferred to align with western ideals. The US feared that if Vietnam fell to communism, it would have a domino effect across Asia and that would not do. So in they jumped (along with other western allies), supporting the South Vietnamese, while the Russians and Chinese banded together with the communist north. Twenty years of fighting against the communist north did not leave the southerners much inclined to accept a name change of their beloved capital. Given all the history, it’s not surprising that there’s a bit of tension between the north and the south just below the surface and it extends from religion to history to language. In spite of all this, the people of the south are every bit as smiling, friendly and peaceful as those in the north – even when they’re stuck in rush hour traffic! The war museum is a very difficult place to visit but is a necessary stop on your itinerary. With its fear of communism, America wreaked havoc on a poor country that didn’t do anything wrong. Two thirds of the three million Vietnamese killed in the war were civilians. After the war. Another 100,000 were killed or injured by unexplored bombs and land mines between 1975 and 2002. The devastation inflicted on the Vietnamese people was not just from bombs and guns; starting in 1961 and for a period of ten years, the US conducted just under 2,000 missions spraying 80 million litres of toxic chemicals (61% of which was Agent Orange) over 26,000 villages (25% of the area of South Vietnam). Nearly five million Vietnamese were exposed to Agent Orange which claimed three million victims at the time. This chemical had devastating and lethal effects on all parts of the human body and caused genetic and chromosome mutations resulting in birth defects and reproductive issues…a domino effect Eisenhower could never have dreamed of. Victims of Agent Orange continue to be claimed four generations since Agent Orange was sprayed over South Vietnam, totalling at least 187,000 but that is probably an underestimate.
