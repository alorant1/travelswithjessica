London, England

London, United Kingdom September 2025 Back to London Here for a short visit before spreading our wings around the UK and Scotland. Thanks, London for picking the exact days we were in London to stage one of your most disruptive strikes ever! A striking tube didnt stop us though, just made getting around a little more time consuming and served to line the pockets of the taxi and uber drivers who were most happy to pick up the slack. I’m not sure if there were more people commuting to work by bike because of the Tube strikes or if this was typical, but there sure were a lot of bikes on the roads. With the volume of traffic, there was no way I was going to attempt to ride a share bike on the other side of the road to compensate for the lack of Underground service! For the most part, we walked…and walked and walked. And so many interesting things one sees when walking from A to B! Walking through Kensington Park to meet a friend in Mayfair, I came across many people walking their dogs. But this was the first time I’d ever seen anyone out walking their bird! How lovely to see Canada Geese in the minority for once! These ones certainly did not have the attitude of ours in Vancouver who seem to think they own the city. These two have been walking for a week…they started in Oxford and are heading to protest at the DSEI Global Arms Fair. They are almost there; only three hours to go! I don’t think Prince Albert’s memorial is the best seat in the house but he can certainly surveil all the goings on in his dream venue. His wife, Queen Victoria commissioned the Royal Albert Hall in her husband’s memory after he died at the young age of 42. He officially died of Typhoid Fever but apparently had been ailing for a few years beforehand and nowadays medical historians think he likely had Chron’s disease or stomach cancer. Albert had a passion for the arts and this Hall fulfilled his dream of a venue where all people could come together to enjoy music, art and lectures. Victoria was so devastated by his death that she wore black for the remaining 40 years of her life. Trying to be as tall as Evan! I’m on my tippy toes and he is crouching with the eyes of Sotheby’s looking on. How wonderful to catch up with Rachel at Mercato Mayfair! We’ve know each other since the start of high school. “No minute gone comes ever back again. Take heed and see ye nothing do in vain.” Can’t think of more apt words beneath the clock at Liberty. Better hurry up and shop, shop, shop! We waited for ages for the Uber boat to take us to the Tate Modern (in the absence of the Tube). Finally, one came by but didn’t stop. No, it wasn’t full, the water level in the Thames was too low at this spot for it to pull in close enough to the dock. Someone must have opened up a lock or something because a while later, one pulled in…either that or the Uber boat was having its own little solidarity work to rule. The Tate Modern never fails to impress. So many inspiring works throughout this place and lots of interesting things not in the galleries. This is American artist Jenny Holzer’s Inflammatory Essays 1979-1982. She read what she calls “crackpot literature” to find writings expressing extreme political and moral positions. None of these reflect her own beliefs but, in her words, she wanted to ‘instill a sense of urgency in the reader. I wanted the reader to jump, at least, and maybe consider doing something useful.’ Interestingly, these days, with so much extremism in our faces day in and day out, we have been desensitized and don’t react anymore. Korean artist Do Ho Suh created this brand new work, Public Figures, to upturn the traditional patriarchal narrative of commemorating male heroes with statues on plinths. Here, the heroes in the form of hundreds of resin figure are holding the plinth up, a gesture about collective effort, anonymous labor, the weight of memory, and who supports whom. Here’s Public Figures moving itself to its new home. Spent from all that culture and taking a breather on the Millennium Bridge with St Paul’s Cathedral in the background. Now to head back and get ready for a special dinner! So much for getting ready’ the Uber (car) was so stuck in traffic we needed to shift plans and go straight to dinner at Evan’s restaurant, Wild by Tart. He got to be on the customer side tonight. He had worked earlier in the day so we take great pride in knowing he made our desserts tonight. Spectacular meal! They sure take care of their waterfowl here…heaven forbid they should tumble into the pond. Now there’s a lot of share bikes! Are there this many when the Tube is running normally? Evan took us to Miel for coffee. A tiny bakery that punches well above its weight. The coffee and pastries were fabulous. Apparently Nigella comes here whenever she is in town. We finished the afternoon in a pub, of course! Here I was introduced to cask ale. Known as “real” ale, this is unfiltered and unpasteurized beer that continues to mature in the barrel. The secondary fermentation that occurs in the barrel gives it a soft and gentle carbonation with no added CO2. This is not a burp-inducing beer! With no added carbonation, the bartender pumps it out of the barrel by hand. This barrel has a short shelf life and once tapped it needs to be drunk within a few days…not a problem in this pub culture country! February, 2024 Luscious London For me, London has it all! Oxford, England

Oxford, United Kingdom September, 2025 Opulent Oxford We stopped in Oxford mostly to see the Bodleian Library (only a library gets to put three vowels in a row in its name). Sadly It didn’t occur to us that tickets would be in such high demand on a rainy Thursday…Maybe because it was a convocation day and people were sneaking out of the ceremony, or maybe because it’s one of the most famous libraries in the world. Next time… But we still found some things to do in Oxford in the rain! Day trip to Oxford started with lunch at the Covered Market. I’d like to put “Covered” in quotes as when the rain bucketed down outside, we got misted through the roof slats. Classic ivy covered Oxfords. With all that ivy chipping away at the stone it is climbing up, it’s astonishing these buildings haven’t crumbled. Italy follows me everywhere! Oxford’s “Bridge of Sighs” this is actually the Hertford Bridge but got its nickname from is resemblance to the bridge in Venice that linked the courts to the prison cells where those convicted sighed as they crossed the bridge and had their last glimpse of freedom. No courts and prisons are attached to this bridge but perhaps the students have some degree of anxiety as they cross under this bridge on their way to write their exams. Rainy graduation day in Oxford. This dressed up mom, clutching her grad’s backpack, does not look impressed. With the Bodleian library sold out on this rainy Thursday, we ended up seeing an amazing exhibit on Treasures in the Weston Library. The concept was what makes something a treasure. The first of something, the oldest of something, the last of something, something that triggers memories; pleasant or sad. Treasures are what we are made of; they form our past and hopefully guide our present and future. Here are just a few of many items that resonated with me…for a variety of reasons. Famous: This is the Gutenberg Bible. Printed sometime around 1455, it wasn’t the first printed object but it was the first in Europe printed with movable type…and the first best seller! Even though each copy was hand-decorated by artists, printing allowed so many more copies of a book to be made. Oldest: This little pagoda holds one of the world’s oldest printed texts. In the 760s, Empress Shötoku of Japan ordered one million of these wooden pagodas, each with a printed dharani – a sacred Buddhist spell. Craftspeople printed the scrolls, rolled them by hand and placed one inside each pagoda to spread belief and shape identity. I’m not sure if they followed the Empress’s instructions and actually made a million little pagoda scroll holders, but if they did, it would have satisfied the demand of about 20% of the Japanese population of the time. Faith: Soldiers heading into battle often took Qur’ânic verses with them as talismans. This 16th century shirt took that tradition to a whole new level. The entire Qur’ân is printed on it. I see no bloodstains so I would guess this soldier lived to tell his tale. Prototype: This is the original manuscript of Mary Shelly’s Frankenstein. She came up with the idea in 1816 while visiting Lord Byron’s villa on Lake Geneva and wrote it all down in this notebook. Questionable? Here is the first published edition of Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland. This classic has a debated backstory that in modern times is considered a bit dodgy. But times change and to what extent do we have the right to criticize what was acceptable in the past. Lewis Carroll is actually the pen name of Charles Dodson, a mathematician who taught at Christ Church, Oxford. He was friendly with Henry Liddell, the Dean, and his family. He would take Henry’s kids on outings and tell them stories. When he was 30, in 1862, he came up with the story of Henry’s daughter, Alice, falling down a rabbit hole. The rest is a history that is debated in literary circles. Carroll’s relationship with the family abruptly ended in 1863 and there is no record as to why; the pages in the family diary describing this period were torn out. Of course, this is where speculation begins! Carroll, like many of his time, enjoyed the hobby of photographing children in costume or naked. Alice was one of his subjects. Were Carroll’s motivations innocent? He went on to publish Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland a year after the rift. Reflection: Some items remind us of painful truths and uncomfortable histories but we need to treasure and preserve them so that we can stay grounded in reality. The bible on the left “Select Parts of the Holy Bible for Negro Slaves” was printed in 1807. What was selectively removed was all the references to struggles for freedom, for example the Israelites’ escape from slavery in Egypt. Don’t want to give anyone any foolish ideas… This is the Martyr’s Memorial Cross in the middle of Oxford’s Broad Street marks the spot where, in 1555 and 1556, Hugh Latimer, Bishop of Worcester, Nicholas Ridley, Bishop of London, and Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury were burnt at the stake for their faith. Queen Mary I was struggling to bring back Catholicism. These three, the Oxford Martyrs, were prominent Protestant reformers and Mary would have none of it. There are hundreds of miles of public rights of way through private property in the Cotswolds. People on foot, bicycles or horses are free to cross through these fields and farmlands, alongside sheep and cows. This walk started in Chipping Campden, the beginning of the 164km Cotswolds Way which ends in Bath. “Chipping” comes from old English meaning market. Town’s starting with the word Chipping, were designated market towns in the Doomsday Book. We came across references to the Doomsday Book quite a lot. While to me doomsday sounds like the horrible end of something, it was actually the start of something. The book was a survey of all of England commissioned by William the Conqueror in 1085 to record who owned land, how it was used and what it was worth. William wanted the lay of the land of his new kingdom for taxation and control purposes. As a market town, Chipping Campden was known for its wool trade…no kidding! This is one of the most iconic spots in the UK and is the image on the inside cover of the British passport. York, England

York, United Kingdom September, 2025 Yearning for York York was the town that everyone seemed to want for themselves. The Romans got here first in 71AD (as they were wont to do in those days), taking cities for the ever expanding empire. This location proved to be a bit of a poor choice and by 410, Rome pulled out. The empire was running out of money and had bigger fish to fry on the continent. Maintaining a hold on Britain (at a distance separated by water) was expensive. For the next few hundred years, this little city in the north was heralded as “the second city” (after London) controlling the north of England and became a site of many skirmishes among the power-seekers of the day: the Anglo Saxons, the Vikings and the Normans and various Kings who came and went. And that’s how you get a city today with 2,000 years of history popping up wherever you go! Beside York Minster sits Constantine the Great. He was declared Roman Emperor in 306AD and was instrumental in establishing the Christian faith in the western world. After having a religious vision and winning his following battle, he decided there was something to this religion. It was also politically advantageous to ditch paganism and side with what was proving to be the winning team among both the rich and the poor. To symbolize this, he cut off the pointy end of his sword which turned the weapon into a cross (as depicted in this statue). Not unlike many others, he was baptized on his deathbed…this way he could be cleansed of his sins when there was no chance of sinning any more. York Minster is one of the most iconic spots in York. It all started here on this spot when the Pagan King Edwin of Northumbria wanted to marry Ethelburgh, Christian princess of Kent. Part of the deal was that he consider converting to Christianity. He tabled it for a while and when he was heading off to battle some rival kingdoms, he asked her to pray for his success. If he won, he would convert. Well, he came home alive and two kingdoms richer so he kept to his word, converted in 627AD and commissioned a wooden church to be built on this site where York Minster now stands. After his death in 633, his little church fell into disrepair but the location was set. Stone churches replaced the wooden one and in 1220 they started building what we know today as York Minster. It took 250 years to build and was finally completed in 1472. When you look at the front of the church, you notice a very clean and white statue to the right of the main door. It’s Queen Elizabeth II, installed in August 2022 marking her platinum Jubilee. The other thing you notice is all the other niches are empty. They likely never had statues in them as the plinths are bare with no evidence of rods to hold anything in place. It turns out cost overruns shifted the construction priorities, not to mention the Plague hitting York in 1349 and killing much of the population including stonemasons and carvers. By the 19th century, there was more money to invest in major renovations and they could have added statues but decided to leave the niches empty to keep the facade cleaner and not distract from the soaring architecture. While I was busy admiring the front of the minster and wondering about all these questions, Adam was busy making a few new friends who insisted on taking a photo with him. Moving onto inside the Minster, most niches are also empty. Some were never filled and others’ statues were destroyed or damaged during the Reformation. This is part of the 2003 installation of Semaphore Saints by artist Terry Hammill. The semaphore system of communication uses hand-held flags in different arm positions as letters of the alphabet. This sculpture spells out “Christ is Here”. Using semaphores was a symbolic nod to the Minster’s medieval stonemasons’ carvings to convey stories and morals for the illiterate. No sooner did our guide explain that if you were a Scout or Guide as a child you may have learned this set of hand signals, I noticed an older couple beside me working out the message letter by letter. They are headless because we are meant to focus on the message. In the back, on the quire screen, are some statues! These are all the kings from William the Conqueror on the top left to Henry IV on the bottom right (top row is left side of the door, bottom row is the right side of the door). These fierce looking kings are guarding the clergy, using their royal power to defend the church. Of course when these were carved, the kings were long gone and the stonemasons didn’t really know what they looked like. If you look closely, you’ll notice a bunch of them look quite similar. The Lake District, England

The Lake District, United Kingdom September, 2025 Lounging in the Lakes While I loved the Costwolds’ gentleness and rolling hills, the Lake District really drew me in with its mountains, ruggedness and dramatic vistas. Maybe the Herdies also had something to do with it… What? More sheep?! Dry stone walls are all over the UK (estimates are that they run a distance of 180,000-200,000 miles). These are definitely not a “seen one, seen ‘em all” kind of thing. They can date as far back as medieval times and were used to keep sheep and cattle where they belonged and mark property lines. The older ones were often built with the slate uncovered when farmers cleared the land for grazing. Newer ones (by new, we’re talking 18th century) more often than not used rounder, volcanic rocks and sometimes mixed them with slate. And no mortar was used to build these long-standing walls; just stones carefully placed with precision by someone who really knows what they are doing! There seems to be no end to these walls and no terrain they can’t climb. The older ones don’t tend to run in straight lines. These were not easy to build so they often ran the path of least resistance. Herdies! These are the sweetest sheep of all! The Herdwick sheep are only found in this region as they have adapted to handle the extreme weather of the Lake District. They can survive on grazing alone and their extra thick fleece (which runs down their legs and along their long tails) keeps them warm and dry in the cold and wet winters. They change colour as they grow! They are born all black and as they age, their faces turn white and their wool fades to brown. After their first shearing (at about 12-18 months) their fleece grows back grey and stays that way. This extra thick wool is quite coarse and not ideal for sweaters but it is perfect for carpets, tweeds and insulation. Needless to say, the value of the wool is a lot lower but there has been a movement to preserve and protect this iconic creature of this region. We can thank Beatrix Potter for the Herdies we see today. She saved them from decline by farming them and when she died, she left them and her land to the National Trust. So she wasn’t only about rabbits messing about in vegetable patches! Herdies are often left to roam freely in the uplands without walls to keep them penned in. The flock is “hefted” to a particular patch of fell. It’s their forever home and they pass this homing instinct down through the generations. The lambs follow their mothers around and learn their boundaries…unlike human children. Sprinkled in with the Herdies on our hike from Little Langdale past Blea Tarn, a stepped waterfall. The Herdies seem to stay away from the water. Smart. Ewes must have taught their young this important lesson, along with where home is. You can spot us miles away! Edinburgh, Scotland

Edinburgh, Scotland September, 2025 Enigmatic Edinburgh Ah Edinburgh, such a beautiful city yet so difficult to pronounce for most. Like so many names of places in the UK, the letters belie the pronunciation. This city, with a bit of nature sprinkled in, verges on perfect for me. The people are so lovely, the history is rich (and poor!), the coffee and food fabulous and the stories and lore somehow believable. The only absence seems to be the sheep! We’d barely arrived and I found myself faced with haggis. If I can eat crickets in a taco in Mexico, ants garnishing a dish in Vietnam and chicken sashimi in Japan, how could I, in good conscience, skip out on haggis in Scotland? It was actually quite good, especially since it was a bit bougie’d up as a haggis bonbon…chased with a wee dram of whisky. And if you’re questioning my spelling of this classic Scottish beverage…with or without an “e”, both are correct. In Scotland and Canada, we can’t be bothered with the “e” and in Ireland and the US, they slip in the “e”; odd in American English, given they are prone to dropping letters they deem unnecessary. And in case you’re interested in creating this classic Scottish delicacy yourself, we found this copy of the first printed recipe from 1773. If you know a farmer, you should be able to get all the ingredients you need. Edinburgh is full of these closes, narrow alleys leading off the main road, into courtyards behind the buildings or linking to the streets behind. This was a walled city so as the population grew, they were forced to build up, sometimes reaching 10-12 storeys. The rich lived among the poor but they got the breezy top floors with the poor relegated to the damp, dark, lower levels. This made old Edinburgh one of the most densely populated places in 17th century Europe. The advantage of these socially-mixed residential areas was if the poor had a problem, so did the rich so issues were more likely to be addressed. But these crammed quarters became a hive of disease. The rich complained which led to the city building out the New Town (“New” as in 1767). The rich moved out to wide streets and bright open spaces and left the Old Town to the poor. The Royal Mile was the one wide street in old Edinburgh book ended by Holyrood Palace at one end and the Edinburgh Castle at the other. Its name is distance-accurate in old Scottish terms. Before the Acts of Union in 1707 which united the kingdoms of Scotland and England into Great Britain, Scotland had its own system of weights and measures. A Scottish mile was 1/8th (220 yards) longer than the English Mile. Edinburgh’s Royal Mile is 24 yards short of the Scottish mile. The length was coincidental but it made for a good name! In the middle of the Royal Mile, right beside the St Giles Cathedral, stands a statue of Adam Smith a famous Scott who wrote Wealth of Nations. How fitting that he’s looking up the street at the Tron Kirk (the spire with the clock tower), originally a mid-1600s Church of Scotland parish church. The church was nicknamed for the “tron”, a public weighing beam where merchants arriving in Edinburgh to sell their goods like grain, butter, coal, salt and meat had to have them officially weighed for market. This ensured fair taxation and pricing. The wealth of the nation played out before him. As for the traffic cone on his head…a classic college prank, on repeat. The church closed for worship in the 1950s and was converted into the Tron Craft Centre in the 1980s. The wealth of the nation continues to be supported by local artisans selling their wares to tourists, locals and their dogs. Luckily, St Giles Cathedral, perfectly positioned in the middle of the Royal Mile, is still open for business. Built in the 12th century, King David I decided to dedicate his cathedral to a highly popular saint in medieval Europe. Giles was from Provence and never actually set foot in Edinburgh, let alone Scotland, but became Edinburgh’s patron saint anyway. His cultish fame as patron of lepers, beggars and the disabled spread through the medieval church’s network of saints’ veneration so King David picked him. As the main parish church of Edinburgh, and as Edinburgh became Scotland’s capital, the cathedral became the hub of all things important like royal processions and proclamations, civic ceremonies and big state events. As such, St Giles as well as the cathedral became nationally important. John Knox, the Scottish Reformer, preached here in the 1560s so it became seen as the heart of Scotland’s new national religion. When Queen Elizabeth II died, her coffin lay at rest here due to her special connection to Scotland and the fact that she attended services here when she was in town. Fort William, Scotland

Fort William, Scotland September, 2025 Wild Fort William We left the big city of Edinburgh for some hiking in the Scottish countryside. Some important stops on the way in and out broke up the driving. The Falkirk Wheel is an engineer-at-heart husband’s paradise. Nestled about 45 minutes from Edinburgh sits this remarkable marvel. Here’s the backstory. Scotland has two major canals: the Union Canal which links Edinburgh and Falkirk; and the Forth & Clyde Canal which crosses central Scotland from the Firth of Forth (east) to the Firth of Clyde (west). In the 19th century, they were linked by a series of 11 locks opening up water transport between Edinburgh and Glasgow. Modern transportation like the railway and road network improved and left the canal transportation network in the dust. In 1933, the locks were dismantled and the two canals were cut off from each other. In the 1990s, the Millennium Link Project undertook the huge task of restoring and reconnecting Scotland’s canals for leisure and tourism. The challenge to reconnect the Union Canal with the Forth & Clyde Canal without rebuilding the old staircase of locks to manage the 35m difference in height was solved by the Falkirk Wheel, which opened in 2002.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/Mxl72I8G/falkirk-wheel.movThe Falkirk Wheel was the brainchild of architect Tony Kettle. £78 million later, Falkirk became home to world’s one and only rotating boat lift. Each of the two huge gondolas (also called caissons) are 25m x 8m and can carry up to four boats each, depending on their size, of court. Part of the magic of these 500 litre gondolas is rooted in Archimedes’ principle which shows that an object placed in a fluid displaces its own weight of that fluid. So the two gondolas’ weight of 500 tonnes never changes, regardless of how many boats may be in them. The gondolas are always in perfect balance even if one has no boats and the other is full. And because of this perfect balance, the wheel only requires the energy equivalent to boil 8 kettles (of course that is the analogy, we are in the UK after all) to overcome the friction and get things moving. What once took a full day to navigate through the flight of 11 locks now takes less than 15 minutes from sailing in from once canal to sailing out into the other! This is the Union Canal at the top of the Falkirk Wheel. It’s a pretty stunning sculptural view even without considering the engineering marvel that it is! A ten-minute drive up the road from the Falkirk Wheel, sitting alongside the the Forth & Clyde Canal is another marvel; the Kelpies. Created by Scottish sculptor Andy Scott, these two 30m tall steel horse-head sculptures are an engineering feat themselves. In Scottish folklore, a kelpie is a shape shifting water spirit that often appears as a horse, luring people to their watery demise. It’s a great story to keep kids away from the dangers of water! But the symbolism of these kelpies is not only rooted in folklore. They are also a nod to the work horses essential to life in Scotland’s past, not only important in agriculture but workhorses also pulled barges along the canals. The Kelpies each weigh about 300 tonnes with nearly 1,000 steel plates wrapped around a structural steel inner framework. The stainless steel plates are shaped and angled to suggest muscle, tension and flowing manes, capturing the energy of the horse. Creating these magnificent creatures was not quick. From start to finish, the project took about seven years. The design phase lasted two years, the fabrication of the steel structure and plates another five years and then several months of on-site assembly. The Kelpies were officially opened to the public in 2014. Moving on from the magic of engineering to the magic of the sky. The sun is seeking its rest at the end of the day. Isle of Skye, Scotland September, 2025 Swathed by the Isle of Skye The Isle of Skye is a place to behold. The weather leaves little to be desired but the landscape, wildlife and distilleries make up for it! Scotland’s national flower, the beautiful thistle, is all over Scotland. Lovely to look at but I sure wouldn’t want to step on it without shoes. One wonders how a country goes about picking its national flower. I would have thought Scotland might have picked heather as it’s really everywhere, painting the hills and mountains purple in late summer/early fall (sadly we missed the full bloom by a few weeks). Alas, it seems the choice is rooted in a bit of lore mixed with a dose of symbolism. Legend has it that during a night raid by Norse invaders in 1263, the unsuspecting invaders tried to sneak across a field barefoot to surprise the Scots. One of them stepped on a prickly thistle and cried out in pain, alerting the Scottish defenders who quickly thwarted the attack. Another case of inappropriate footwear in Scotland but it cemented the notion of the thistle saving the nation. Symbolically, this humble weed, with its prickly spines, represents toughness, resilience, and protection — a small, modest plant that’s hard to crush, thriving in poor soils and harsh climates, much like the strong and resilient Scottish people.
