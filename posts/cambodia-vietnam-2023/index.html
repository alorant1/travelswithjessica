Mekong Delta, Vietnam

Mekong Delta, Vietnam March, 2023 Magical Mekong Delta The Mekong River is 5,000 km long, starting in Tibet and running through China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and finally ending in Vietnam. It is said to be the world’s largest inland fishery (25% of the global freshwater catch) but it is also one of the most polluted from excessive amounts of garbage being dumped into it. Pretty much all the fish we’ve been eating in southern Vietnam comes from the Mekong… it is a stunningly beautiful place with wonderful people who are willing to share the stories of their lives and livelihoods. Sun rising over the Mekong River; the fishermen are already well into their day. This is a very fertile area and absolutely nothing goes to waste. Here a bang grass field is being harvested. Grown in muddy water, this thinner bang grass is used to make rope and for weaving. There is also a thicker, hollow variety from a different region that they use to make eco-friendly straws. Added benefit is you can chew the straw after use to clean your teeth! There is a multi-stage process for drying the grass. After it’s cut, it is laid out in the field for “two suns” before being bundled and fanned out for the final drying. A most lovely field worker twisting the grass into ties to tie up the bundles. She couldn’t believe my age…I guess a relatively cushy life shows. Adam interrupting a woman who was fanning out the grass bundles for the final drying. Back to work.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/Z5hGWRd5/img_2579.mp4This gentleman is a Viet Cong veteran. He trained as a nurse and never intended to be a soldier. But as things intensify during a long and hard war, he was “asked” to fight. At the age of 24, he came home for a visit and accidentally touched a land mine which blew off most of the fingers of his right hand. He is 75 and still sings Viet Cong songs and wears his military uniform. Despite everything he’s been through, he remains a believer. He’s playing the mandolin here but he also plays a number of other guitar-like instruments in addition to the harmonica. This lovely woman showed us how to make poon lights by mashing an oily nut with soft plant fibres that looks like the inside of a bulrush. She uses part of a spent bomb casing from the war as her mortar. The finished poon light lasts about 15 minutes and would be used to light your way home after dark. Next she showed us how to make roofing “shingles” from water coconut (growing in the mangrove behind her). She cut off a giant leaf and then used a giant knife to cut off the leaflets. After selecting the best leaflets, she used that really big knife to cut a bamboo stalk in half which became the frame of the shingle. We folded the leaflets over the bamboo and sewed them together using thin, dried strips of an immature leaf, that has not yet split into leaflets, which is strong and sharp enough to act as it’s own needle. While the sleeping part of her house has a corrugated metal roof (with some solar panels mounted on it), she uses these shingles for the cooking area to enable the cooking smoke to escape. This roof will last 20 years! She also had a pair of gas burners powered by an at-home biofuel system. Nothing wasted: The cows and pigs out back provided the necessary ingredient (manure). She was quite an independent woman, standing in for her husband who was crashed, shirtless on the floor, after drinking too much all day at a local wedding.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/XWZKPk06/my-movie-sd-480p.mp4This was fun! This family makes pop rice. The video (credit goes to Adam) shows the process from beginning to final product. He started with 1.5 or 2 kg of rice and the end result was fantastic! The rice takes about 20 minutes in the war era fire extinguisher “pressure cooker” so the wife took a break from the caramel she was making (her husband has to constantly turn the rice device) to offer us some homemade jackfruit moonshine. Did that ever pack a punch! And I lived to tell the tale! Collecting water coconut leaves. These small boats enjoy such serenity along the shore…until they are jockeying for position among the big boats in the middle of the river. A bird nest factory…for bird’s nest soup. A speaker on top of the building plays the swallow song to attract the special birds inside where they build their nests along the walls. These birds don’t collect twigs to make their nests; rather they are made of their own solidified saliva. Needless to say, they are cheaper to find and harvest from a building like this than in the wild but they still sell for a few thousand dollars per kilo for the highest quality ones. They are cooked in water to make a gelatinous soup full of health benefits in the traditional Chinese (and Vietnamese) medicine world. This will cure and improve anything and everything from libido, immune function, mental focus, respiratory ailments and digestive problems. Of course, some say it cures Covid… Another local market where we found Vietnamese cherries! The many hats of the ladies at the local market. Being Sunday, some of the shoppers were quite dressed up! The Cao Dai ( Left Eye) temple on the shore of the Mekong River. The left eye is special because it is closer to the heart and can see wherever you go. This peaceful religion is quite young, having been founded in 1926. It unifies Taoism, Confucianism, Buddhism and Christianity. One big, happy family! We saw lots of coconuts roadside and piled on barges heading up and down the river. Eighty percent of the land here is used to grow coconuts and remember, nothing is wasted! Can Tho, Vietnam

Can Tho, Vietnam March, 2023 Captivating Can Tho Our final few days in Vietnam was spent in a stunning resort on the shores of the Mekong River in Can Tho. The resort was only accessible by boat and was a perfect R&R to a busy three weeks in this beautiful country. But there’s only so much R&R we are inclined to do…markets, monks and more markets! A borrowed outfit from the lovely head monk at a Khmer temple. In 35C heat plus humidity, I shudder to think of how many others sweated in this outfit before me! The head monk took us up to the fourth floor to show us his library under the eaves. He was very proud to show us his 100-year-old Sanskrit texts. The library was full of books, Buddhist and others, with walls covered in colourful artwork. A modern and practical monk has pockets in his robes…a place to put his iPhone and other necessities. He blessed us on the way out and gave us each beautiful orange bracelets. What I didn’t ask the monk was why I had to cover up and she didn’t… From the serenity of the temple to the hustle and bustle of the food street market. Aways wash your vegetables before you eat them…they may have been in someone’s armpit. Repackaging rice into “smaller” bags. So many different kinds of white rice! If you don’t have a storefront, just spread your wares on the street. And we won’t discuss the safety of the child on the motorcycle’s pink “helmet”… Knife seller…don’t want to have road rage with this guy! Nap time, bug zapper at the ready. Love this basket! Can’t imagine how heavy it would be when full. Nap time again! Glad she wasn’t away to ask what she was selling…not sure I want to know what was sitting unrefrigerated on her table. The retail section of the market. The wholesale section of the market. I thought this Buddha follower was showing off his piercing but it turns out Khai Tâm La hán is showing us his heart open to Buddha. Mani pedi time! For all the sneaky pictures I take of people around and about, I caught the spa staff taking pictures of us! Adam chickened out on his pedicure… This barge carrying a crane must have been in a bit of a rush. It didn’t wait for the tide to be low enough and got stuck under the bridge. They broke off a cover that was the high point and they managed to get through. We took an early morning boat trip to the Cai Rang Floating Market. Opening at dawn, it’s pretty much wrapped up by 9am, so we received a bougie breakfast on the way! Cai Rang means “terra cotta pots” which the Khmer people used to sell in this area and became the name of this district in Can Tho. This floating market was started in the early 1900s when the road system was not developed. Despite the very developed roads now, the floating market remains a bustling place. Filling up time at the floating gas station. A once a week fill up of 150L does it for our boat. We were followed by a little boat who comes two or three times per week to fill up with five litres. Note the hammock for resting between customers. Tossing pineapples two at a time from seller to buyer. This is the pineapple area of the market and all the sellers price fix. Not sure how they agree on prices but the message seems to get to those who need to know. The big boats buy directly from the farmers, or are the farmers themselves who load up from their farms upriver (sometimes hundreds of km away) and come here to sell. They will stay until they sell out. This longan and mangosteen seller rocked up to our boat and tied on! How’s that for a sales tactic. She passed us delicious samples and tried to sell us a bag of a dozen of each fruit for the equivalent of $1.50. We were leaving the next day and really couldn’t take it. She was totally fine with our refusal and was either happy to have met us or is laughing at us. I’ll go with the first… Watermelon pick up and then we are ready to go! It’s pretty clear what this boat is selling with its artfully stacked (what if a wave comes?) watermelons visible on the deck. But what if you’re unable to show your wares? Or your wares are not as pretty as watermelons or pineapples? Attach your wares, in this case sweet potato, to a long bamboo pole for all to see! No calling out at this market; you’d never be heard over the din of the boats. This one is a veritable supermarket! She’s got lettuce, sweet potatoes, onions, shallots and garlic. Loaded with watermelons, sweet potatoes and squash and heading to one of many land markets to resell to the vendors there. Big boats, medium-sized boats and small ones. There’s room for everyone but that little one in the middle looks like it’s close to sinking! Those are mighty big engines driving those little propellers. Cozy with our little mangosteen. While waterfront property commands a premium in our real estate markets, here it’s the cheapest property you can buy. One of the Dutch Ocean Clean Up boats along the Mekong River. This is Interceptor 3, installed just over a year ago and designed for heavily trafficked areas with varying currents. It’s removing 10,000kg of plastic per month! A good test site as the Mekong River is one of the most polluted. Good night and tạm biệt beautiful Vietnam. Previous PostNext Post

Phnom Penh, Cambodia

Phnom Penh, Cambodia March, 2023 Tragic Phnom Penh Phnom Penh should be on everyone’s Cambodian travel itinerary. Not because it’s a beautiful city (it’s not), not because the people are lovely and charming (some are), but because its memorials dedicated to the brutality of the Khmer Rouge from 1975-1979 must be seen and felt. Outside of those very difficult visits, what really struck us most was the vibe of the people here as compared to the Vietnamese. The Vietnamese we met were incredibly warm and happy people. They didn’t have much but they seem genuinely happy and interested in connecting with foreigners. There’s an edge to many of the Cambodians we met. They didn’t seem content. There’s begging in the street which we didn’t see at all in Vietnam. I have a couple of theories. One is that the Khmer Rouge wreaked havoc on innocent people…on its own people and it wasn’t very long ago, damaged their collective psyche. I really think there’s probably only one or zero degrees of separation between this population and those who suffered during the 1970s and its aftermath. Another theory is that this seems to be a much more visible gap between the haves and have nots. There are way more fancy cars on the road, way more high end clothing, jewellery and design stores. While the bulk of the population is poor, it seems their noses are rubbed in it much more so than the Vietnamese. No corroboration, just my opinion… Chao Ponhea Yat high school was used by the Khmer Rouge from 1975-1979 as Security Prison 21 (S21), one of between 150-200 interrogation and torture centres. The last 14 victims are buried here on the site of brutal torture of innocents. Pol Pot (not his real name…it’s a nickname meaning the Cambodian”) was born to wealthy landowners and went to Catholic private school. He then went to France on scholarship but seemed to be more interested in crafting his form of radical communism than studying radio electronics. He failed and came back to Cambodia without a degree. Not something to be proud of as a scholarship recipient of strict parents. The Khmer Rouge arrived in Phnom Penh on April 17, 1975 and within three days, the city of 2 million was emptied. The Khmer Rouge was striving for a cashless communist state based on a rural agrarian economy. People were sent to work in the fields with no rest and little food at “collective farms” but really it was forced labour where many were literally worked to death. Pol Pot isolated the Cambodians from the external world, abolished money, private property and religion. Pretty much anything could result in a person being sent to S21 or one of the other torture centres: city folk, intellectuals, wearing glasses or having soft hands. A couple not consummating a forced marriage (need to grow a loyal population fast!)…they were watched…would result in a death penalty. Interestingly, he exterminated all teachers…perhaps because he was an academic failure? The site has been converted into the Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum The torture was so unbearable that prisoners made false confessions to end it. The Khmer Rouge used existing equipment at the school, such as this gym equipment, for torture, in addition to classic torture techniques such as water boarding, sleep deprivation, pulling finger nails out, electric shock and “medical experiments” without anaesthesia. It’s just so hard to imagine how people could blindly follow such evil but they would have been killed if they didn’t… The buildings were wrapped in electrified barbed wire to prevent the desperate prisoners from escaping or jumping to their deaths. This type of cell was reserved for high ranking officers. The Khmer Rouge went after anyone they felt was a threat, from ordinary people and peasants to former government officials and even those thought to be disloyal among their own ranks. People were shackled using these irons and were left with a box for their excrement, which they were often forced to eat Most people were held in these single cells, either built out of wood or brick. Slightly larger cells were for women with babies. No talking allowed. An estimated 20,000 people were tortured in this one location between 1975-1979, including some Westerners such as journalists. All were then put into trucks in the dead of night and taken to what they were told was new housing. In reality they were sent to an extermination camp called Choeung Ek, also known as the Killing Fields. The dips in the ground are the remaining evidence of the mass graves at Choeung Ek, one of 393 killing fields of the Khmer Rouge. Every few weeks 50-70 people were brought here from S21 on trucks and were killed the night they arrived. By 1978 trucks, came every day with as many as 300 people. Bullets were expensive so the regime used everyday tools to beat people to death. Pol Pot said it was “better to kill an innocent by mistake than spare an enemy by mistake”. DDT was scattered over dead bodies to get rid of the smell so as to not arouse suspicion of people living and working nearby. Another reason was the chemical would kill anyone who was still alive after being thrown into the mass grave. This tree was used to kill babies and children by smashing their heads against it. Why children? Entire families were arrested and killed so that nobody would be left alive to seek revenge. When the Khmer Rouge was pushed out by the Vietnamese and the Killing Fields was discovered, 89 of the mass graves were dug up to give the victims a proper burial. The rest were left, some say to not further disturb the victims, while others say the government doesn’t want to acknowledge the full extent of the genocide. Siem Reap

Siem Reap March, 2023 Siem Reap…Who Knew? Wow! Going to Siem Reap, we figured, was all about Angkor Wat but as we discovered, there’s so much more. Shrines, floating villages and ants! Buying lotus flower offerings outside the shrine of Preah Ang Chek Preah Ang Chorm.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/DNfqrVwM/img_3291.mp4Every day, traditional pin piet ensembles play for the hundreds of devoted followers who come to pray to Preah Ang Chek and Preah Ang Chorm who are believed to bring good fortune to the newly married and provide spiritual protection for Siem Reap. Kneeling before Preah Ang Chek and Preah Ang Chom, believed to be two magical sisters who would grant the wishes of those who prayed to them. These girls have had quite a journey to reach their shrine. Originally installed in Angkor Wat in the 16th century, Dap Chhuon, the governor of Siem Reap in the 1950s, raided the site and stole the statues. Black magic helped Dap Chhuon and his five soldiers carry these incredibly heavy, bronze pieces to his headquarters. Before he was executed for trying to overthrow the monarchy, he tried to run away with his “sisters” to Thailand. Sadly, he had lost his magical powers and could no longer carry them by himself. The sisters were recovered and then their journey gets a bit murky (literally). One story says they were blown up by Pol Pot and another (the official one) says they were thrown into the depths of the Siem Reap River from which they were dredged in 1982 and enshrined here opposite the Independence Gardens and the Royal Residence where the King of Cambodia stays when he’s in Siem Reap.. Local women giving offerings and praying to the Leper King. Our guide told us a funny story about his own experience with this shrine. He needed a motorcycle and decided to pray to the Leper King. He promised that if he got a motorcycle, he would continue to share the name of the god. A short time later, he was at an event where a motorcycle was the draw prize. He won! He prayed to the Leper King in thanks and even made an offering of a roasted pig! Surely that would put him in the good books. But he neglected to continue to share his name. Needless to say, he and his girlfriend at the time eventually broke up and he lost the motorcycle in the split. I guess the gods are listening… I guess they don’t make scooter helmets in children’s sizes. I guess public nap time is a thing in Cambodia as well as Vietnam. Of course Adam needed to climb up on this Siem Reap river water wheel to figure out how it worked! Local delicacy…chicken marinated in crushed red tree ants. Don’t believe me? This one missed the crushing, I guess. They’re pretty small so you don’t notice any texture. Taste? Sour, like a lime. A lotus field. Every part of this beautiful plant is used. The flower and leaf for decoration, the root, the seed and the fruit for eating. Apparently the fibres of the plant are also woven into fabric that is more luxurious than silk. We didn’t see any…probably a good thing. Life on the river. The Siem Reap river is as brown and mucky as it looks. The locals swim and fish in it anyway, which offends my sterile, North American sensibilities. For washing, they can drop a chemical in a barrel of water which causes the dirt to drop to the bottom of the barrel and leave clear water on the top…Clear water, not clean water. The Siem Reap river flows into the Tonle Sap lake which ultimately joins up with the Mekong River in Phnom Penh…in the dry season. Oddly, during the wet season, the flow changes direction and the Tonle Sap lake swells to six times its dry season size! Every now and then, our boat driver had to stop and clear the propeller of garbage. The floating village, Chong Kneas. This village is not unlike any other village except for the fact that it’s on the water and only accessible by boat. The 2,000 person community here is mostly made up of Vietnamese immigrants from generations ago, and then others came, escaping the Vietnam war or the Khmer Rouge. According to Cambodian law, immigrants can’t own land in the country so the ever ingenious Vietnamese got around this by living on the water! Remember how the French in Vietnam levied taxes on boats so the Vietnamese made floating baskets which didn’t qualify as boats? Everything you expect in a village is here: houses, shops, a school, a police station, restaurants and even gift shops for tourists! Can’t escape it! The houses are tethered to these bamboo moorings and they float around them as the water moves. Amazing they don’t bump into each other. When the weather is bad in wet season, the houses are untethered and moved into the more protected Siem Reap river. We stopped at a floating restaurant and crocodile farm. This was a small one with maybe a dozen crocs. There are others around the village and a big one in Siem Reap. This is the granddaddy here at the ripe old age of 17 years. He sure looks more like a granddaddy than a teenager. He is kept in a separate pen for breeding…I think he’d be more successful if he cleaned up a bit The crocs need to be about 7-10 years old before they use them for meat and skins. I’m not sure about the business case for this type of farming anymore. Demand has dropped off as bringing in crocodile products is illegal in many countries. Angkor Wat, Cambodia

Angkor Wat, Cambodia March 2023 Astounding Angkor I truly am at a loss for an adjective to describe the Angkor  Archaeological Park. It is mind-blowing and awe-inspiring. What’s really astonishing is how we tourists can still stroll through this ancient and crumbling place…I suspect like other historical sites, it won’t be long before it is placed behind barriers to be admired from afar.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/8Og2bbNS/img_3347.mp4Angkor Wat has been on Adam’s travel bucket list for decades! We left the hotel on bikes at 4:30 am for a 6:15 sunrise. I thought it might be dodgy riding in the dark but there was so much activity roadside! Stands and markets selling fruit and veg and who knows what else were bustling. Now I know why there’s so much public napping! By the time we got there, the sky was a pale, dusty pink and I figured we missed it, even though it was 5:30. Then this happened, right on time! So worth the early wake up. What a magical moment. Many people, us included, think of Angkor Wat as one big temple. Actually, Angkor Wat is one temple among 72 major temples and several hundred minor ones. At 400 acres (four times the size of Vatican City), Angkor Archaeological Park is the largest religious monument in the world. Built between the ninth and 13th centuries, the city of Angkor was the seat of the Khmer empire until it was abandoned in the fifteenth century. Angkor Wat itself took 30 years to build. It is a maze of five central Hindu shrines; we are partway down the 200m bridge that leads to the site. Although it was originally built as a Hindu temple during the 12th century by King Suryavarman II, it gradually transformed into a Buddhist temple by the 13th century when the next king, Jayavarman VII, felt that the Hindu gods had failed him. He built Angkor Thom and the Bayon as a Buddhist temple and then converted Angkor Wat. Ta Reach, at the western gate, is an eight-armed, three metre tall statue of Vishnu to whom locals pray for happiness. I’d be happy too if I had eight arms; life would be so much easier but I’d need to move to a sleeveless wardrobe. The temple walls are covered in 1,200 square metres of bas relief sculptures depicting Hindu gods, ancient Khmer scenes and mythical stories. The Ramayana story we saw at the Royal Palace in Phnom Penh is repeated here…but based on chronology, this one came first so, technically, it’s repeated in the Palace. We later visited an artisan workshop where the artists were carving similar panels by hand out of sandstone. A two foot by four foot panel takes three months to create…no wonder it took 30 years, 300,000 workers and six thousand elephants not only to build but to quarry the necessary five million tons of sandstone from the holy mountain of Phnom Kulen around 50 kilometers away! The scale is incredible. Carvings remain virtually everywhere in Angkor Wat; walls, ceilings, columns and lintels. While some are quite worn, and much has been restored, others are remarkably well-preserved. Gold, silver and jewels as well as most statues have long since been looted or destroyed.… The bridge over the 100m moat leading to the South Gate of Angkor Thom. Fifty-four statues of Devas (benevolent gods) flank one side of the bridge, pulling on the mythical naga serpent. The other side has 54 Asuras (demons) pulling on the naga, representing the endless tug of war between good and evil. The not-so-serene Asuras (demons), grimacing on the other side of the bridge leading to Angkor Thom. The Bayon Khmer Buddhist temple in the centre of Angkor Thom, dates from the late 12th or early 13th century. Serene and smiling faces of Brahma face four ways on each tower. That’s a lot of smiles from 54 towers. It actually felt calming here. The varying heights of the towers represent the peaks of Mount Meru, the center of the Buddhist universe. There are only 37 face-towers left. One of many monkeys, monkeying around Angkor. The grand terrace leading to the Baphuon Temple. The Baphuon Temple is another example of a Hindu temple converted to a Buddhist one. Natural jungle gym. And here come the kids selling bracelets. This little group was busy playing together when suddenly, the most experienced of the crew, noticed a tourist and came running! Receiving a blessing from a monk. We woke him from his afternoon nap for the blessing. I guess this is what makes a monk a monk: he still blessed us. This must be the pinnacle of a monk’s life, to live and study at Angkor, the most important pilgrimage spot in SE Asia. We met a woman of 76 who came from a village far away to visit Angkor for her very first time! Hanging out in a doorway in Preah Khan. Yes, it was hot and the extra layers of modesty coverage were definitely not cooling. Preah Khan has been left pretty much as it was found, aside from shoring up sections for the safety (Cambodian style) of visitors. Lots of trees and other vegetation is growing among the ruins which gives its famous look. Some trees have damaged some structures and have had to be cut down. In the lap of trees consuming the temple walls. One of many well-known trees hugging Preah Khan. If you zoom in, you’ll see two orchids that have taken up residence halfway and three quarters of the way to the top. Spending the day on bikes was surprisingly cooling (if you can even say that) with the bit of wind generated by riding…and the lack of a need for modesty covering. It was swelteringly hot and the humidity was unbelievable. I think this was my hottest ever…and April is going to get even hotter. At least it scared off the crowds.
