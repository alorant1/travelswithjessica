Mexico City

Mexico City, Mexico November, 2023 Mexico City, Round 2! It only took a year to get back to this fabulous city! In a city this big and this amazing, there is always something new to discover…and eat! The secret about Mexico City’s Cablebus is getting out. The two lines completed so far are the two longest gondolas in the world at 9.2 and 10.6 km. Built as part of the transit system, and not as a tourist attraction, these gondolas link neighbourhoods that weren’t served by the metro and substantially shrink the residents’ commute, a big deal in this gridlocked city! In an effort to make them appealing and clean up the areas in full view of the gondolas, the government hired muralists to paint rooftops and upper walls. It’s a really lovely way to commute: beautiful views, fresh air, and even city sounds (dogs barking, music, birds) piped in through the speaker. This time we were prepared enough to book a tour of the remarkable Museum of Anthropology. We had a private tour with Maricela whose praises I couldn’t sing loudly enough. There are classic VW Beetles all over the place here. Apparently Mexico had the last manufacturing site of these beauties, closing in 2003. Why so popular here? Cheap and easy! Not only was the Beetle meant to be an affordable car “for the masses”, in 1988 the government created a tax break, reducing the cost of the cheap car by a further 20%. Mechanically, the Beetle was simple. A sophisticated lawnmower, it was easy to repair. The manual was like a cook book. An old timer taxi driver said he could replace the fan belt with pantyhose! This one, completely covered in seed beads, paying tribute to the Central Mexican Huichol culture, was in a gallery. Eight artisans applied 2,277,000 beads to the car, a project of over 4,000 hours. This could inspire a new beading project for me. I could cover all the dings and scrapes on my old car! Food glorious food…a common refrain for us in Mexico City! Waiting for a blue corn tortilla, made on the spot from a big bin of dough behind the woman standing in this little hole in the wall, stuffed with Oaxaca cheese and chicken. The magical El Parián art-nourveau arcade hidden in the Roma Norte neighbourhood. Now THIS! An absolutely spectacular meal at Expendio de Mais Sin Nombre. Owner Jesús Tornés is an unassuming fellow from the state of Guerrero who has recreated his rural childhood kitchen in this lean-to on a street in Roma Norte. For a hidden, unadvertized restaurant, we waited two hours for our seats. Not a hardship as Roma Norte is a cool neighbourhoods to stroll around. Using traditional clay pots, wood-fired comal (the big, metal griddle we see everywhere in the streets) and very local ingredients, the magic happens. There is no menu, you eat shoulder to shoulder, en famille, with whoever is beside you. You let Jesús know of any dietary restrictions and then the food comes in rounds, with a meat and vegetarian dish to share (assuming you have no restrictions). After three rounds, Jesús asks if you’re done or want more food. Even if you think you’re done, you want more food. We lasted five rounds. I can’t tell you the details of each dish because my Spanish isn’t good enough to grasp the intricacies of the descriptions but wow… Starting our cruise in Xochimilco, on the outskirts of Mexico City, with a michelada. Corona mixed with mango purée and rimmed with tamarind. Don’t knock it till you try it. There’s a reason the cup is so big! When you think of Xochimilco, you think of colourful trajineras navigating tourists around the Floating Gardens in various states of drunkenness, the Mexican Venice. True, sort of. First of all, the Floating Gardens (islands) are not really floating. All of Mexico City is built on a lake and the industrious pre-Hispanic Mexicans needed solid surface areas to live and farm on. They dredged up mud and silt from the bottom of the lake and piled it up until it broke the surface of the water. Then they planted trees with roots that reached all the way down to the bottom of the lake and held the man made islands (Chinampas) in place. The remaining water resulted in a network of canals that served as shipping and transportation routes. All that’s left from those times is what we can visit in Xochimilco, just outside Mexico City. Is it a booze cruise? For some it is, and certainly the evening cruises are solidly about the booze and not so much the views and stops with stories on the way. We went in the afternoon with a fabulous guide and an awesome group of 10. Yup, I was the oldest but I think I held up pretty well! Definitely go as a group or join a tour. The fuller boats are having way more fun. We saw a few of these boats with just a couple of people and a guide and they all looked positively bored. Of course there’s a bar on a hidden island. But not so hidden from the scores of trajineras passing by. Where we were, there’s a fake Island of the Dolls (the real one is a few hours away). Island of the Dolls sounds like a cute, playful place which is a far cry from its true (?) creepy origin. There are surely countless stories about the origins of the Island of the Dolls, but one fact is consistent. Don Julian Santana Barrera was a caretaker of a small island among the canals about two hours from where we were. One day, he discovered a little girl drowned by his island. Chichén Itzá, Mexico

Chichén Itzá, Mexico December, 2023 Remarkable Ruins Chichen Itza is one of the seven new wonders of the world and is one of the most visited archaeological site in Mexico. Sound like a tourist trap? Pretty much, but the trappings are pretty spectacular. In its pre-Columbian-times heyday, 70,000 people lived in this bustling political, commercial and religious power city, surrounding some pretty remarkable temples and pyramids. The iconic Temple of Kukulcán, also known as El Castillo by the Spanish conquistadors, or by its formal archaeological name, Chichen Itza Structure 5B18 (those archaeologists can be so creative). There’s a bunch of real life magic in this pyramid. The mathematicians, astronomers, and architects made a great team here. They built this temple in honour of their chief deity, Kukulcán, the feathered serpent, the creator god, and the god of rain, wind, storms, and life. Watching the skies, they figured out a year was 365 days (and even realized that there was an additional six hours to each year, hence our leap year). This temple was built with 91 stairs on each of the four sides and one additional step into the temple at the top. Total = 365. What we see here is the third temple nested on top of two previous ones. Every 52 years, the 365 day solar cycle would line up with the Mayan 260 named day cycle (some say the 260 days corresponded to the length of pregnancy). Imagine two wheels, one with 365 cogs and the other with 260. Every 52 years, the starting cogs would line up and that would begin another cycle. Using an existing temple or pyramid as a foundation, they would build a new one on top making improvements based on the knowledge gained over the 52 years that had passed. This Temple of Kukulcán has two temple inside it and this temple 3.0 has some amazing new features.https://videos.files.wordpress.com/Ct24Waio/img_0936.movThe ancient Maya were masters of echo. The most sophisticated calculation here is in the precise design of the steps and platforms. If you stand in a particular spot in the large plaza in front of the pyramid and clap your hands, you’ll hear an echo that sounds just like the sacred Quetzal bird. It is a small, colourful bird with one metre long tail feathers. When it flies it looks like a snake (remember the chief deity is a snake). The special feathers were made into headdresses but because the birds were sacred, they were always released after the four long tail feathers were collected. These special tail feathers would regrow every year. These feathers had great monetary value and in Guatemala, to this day, the currency is called the quetzal! Have a listen to our guide, Alfredo, the master clapper. If you’re not standing in the right spot, you can’t hear the echo from your own clapping but someone across the plaza will. Now imagine the plaza full of Maya citizens and three strategically positioned drummers. When they drummed together, everyone, regardless of where they were standing, could hear the call of the quetzal.  The ancient Maya were also master farmers. Their incredible knowledge of the seasons from their deep dives into the study of moon and sun cycles, enabled them to know that the optimal time plant was the Spring Equinox and the perfect time to harvest was the Fall Equinox. How did they share that with the masses? A security guard at Chichen Itza (not the many who made a lifetime study of the site) figured out the secret message the ancients left. At both the Spring and Fall Equinoxes, the rising or setting sun hits the pyramid in such a way that the illumination and shadows along the edge of the pyramid looks like the snake god Kukulcan descending. Since it wasn’t the equinox when I took this photo, I’ve illustrated in green where the illumination would be, from the top left side all the way down to the head of the snake at the base of the stairs on the next wall of the pyramid. Chichen Itza has the largest Ball Game Court in Mesoamerica. Another bit of magical construction here. The acoustics have been studied but nobody has figured out why the sound during play is not at all affected by wind direction or time of day. There was enough room to seat 2,000 on top of the walls surrounding the court. Given the population of 70,000, the seating would have been reserved for the elite. Unlike the Aztecs who could only use their hips to hit the heavy rubber ball through the rings at the centre top of the side walls, the Maya could use their knees and elbows. The engravings on the side walls show the players with knee and elbow padding. The ultimate prize for winning? A trip to heaven! The captain of the winning team indeed gets a fast pass to heaven. Literally. In this relief sculpture, you can see the ball at the centre of the panel with a human skull in the middle. To the right you see the captain of (presumably) the winning team on one knee. The six snakes coming out of where his head should be represent the blood spurting out following his decapitation by the captain of the losing team (who is pictured to the left of the ball holding his opponent’s head in one hand and a knife in the other). Winning the ball game was an honour that won a direct ticket to heaven without having to pass each of the thirteen steps to the peaceful heaven. Getting a boost to get the shot… You can only see Chaac Mool sitting atop the Temple of Warriors if you are a fair distance away. Hence the boost to try to exclude as much of the crowds in front as possible. Chaac Mools were a place to make non-sacrificial offerings to the rain god in hopes of good crops. Valladolid, Mexico

Valladolid, Mexico December, 2023 Vibrant Valladolid Valladolid is one of Mexico’s well-deserved Puebla’s Magicos, officially recognized for its magical qualities of beauty, history and legends. Although touristy, it retains much of its authenticity. Its tourist claim to fame is its proximity to Chichen Itza but most heading to the pyramids make a beeline straight there from Cancun and the rest of the coast and don’t stop in this stunning town. I would come back in a heartbeat and then explore further west to taste the real Mexico. There are just so many beautiful colonial buildings in Valladolid. I had to choose a mere ten from my roll of a thousand! Classic old VW Beetles are all over town in various states of roadworthiness. The famous El Tigrillo taco stand on the corner of calle 41 and calle 44 (easy to navigate a town when all the streets are numbered!). Cochinita pibil tacos and polcanes (fried corn dough shaped like a small football and split open like a bun). Spectacular! Robyn so didn’t want to be in this cringy photo so we just had to do it! These chairs are all over the town and are called “Confidentes” (confidant), “Tú y Yo” (you and me), “De los Enamorados” (for those in love) and in more technical terms “Sillas Binarias” (binary chairs). Perfect for long conversations without getting a crick in your neck! Picking up electrical tidbits. Unintended Mezcaleria selfie. Portico at the former Franciscan Convento de San Bernardino de Siena. This is one of the oldest colonial buildings in Valladolid, dating back to 1552 when its purpose was to convert the indigenous population. It’s still a place of worship but I don’t think we will see any nuns strolling here anymore. Old men sitting in the park sharing their wisdom and undoubtedly solving all the problems of the world. They wouldn’t let me take a picture in front of the Valladolid sign but I sneakily got it, mirror image, in the background. Family ❤️ The beautiful Calle de los Frailes. Purpose-built in the 16th century by the Spanish conquistadors to separate themselves from the local Maya population, this road now is a hip hub of fabulous food and shopping. It definitely has different daytime and nighttime vibes. Hunting for fresh chiccarrón…found it at the Mercado Municipal! And now, for something completely different. The Xkopek Bee Sanctuary. What a treat! This nature park in a dead cenote (yup, there is a life cycle to the cenote, more on that in a bit) was absolutely fascinating. The name comes from two Mayan words: “Xkop” meaning dry/dead cenote and “ek” which is a type of wasp. This park is all about the stingless Melipona bees native to the Yucatan. These bees are about a third the size of bees we are familiar with in North America and look more like flies. There are five Melipona bees that make their protected home in Xkopek. The xnuk build their nests underground and cover up their access holes with earth and propolis when they are out. Because their nest is easily accessed by predators, they actually have a fake, empty nest nearer the surface of the ground to trick predators. The real nest is about 5 m below ground. Another one I don’t have a photo of (probably because it operates in stealth mode) is the limon kaab which don’t actually make honey. They steal pollen and honey from other nests to survive. Their name comes from the lemon scented pheromones they release to make the guard bees “drunk” so they can gain access. Even though they don’t make honey, they support the ecosystem by being active participants in pollination. The Xunan Kab, the Royal Lady Bee, is the most sacred of bees and is believed to be a direct descendent of the Falling God (the god of the earth) who we saw depicted at the ruins of Tulum. These bees build their nests out of wax and propolis. The structure is hard and honey is extracted using a syringe to avoid damaging the cells. These bees make honey with chemicals that ward off microbial and fungal growth, an adaptation to keep the substance from spoiling in the tropics. It’s quite a magical, healing potion! The Sakxik bee (named for the white spot on their wings) don’t produce much honey but focus more on producing propolis, a resin-like waxy substance that is reputed to have a lot of healing properties. The propolis has antiseptic qualities and the bees also use it to prevent the wood of their host tree from rotting. They nest in a hollow tree and like all the others, have a guard bee at the entrance who only lets in his own kind. Not the best photo but it all happened so fast in front of us. Here you can see a guard bee at the entrance of the nest and another Sakxik bringing back two little bundles of yellow pollen. The k’ant’zak is the most aggressive of the community and bites since it can’t sting. This one builds a propolis tube sticking out of a hollow tree to access its nest inside. The black on this tree is actually propolis to seal the wood to prevent rot. This is a Ceiba tree, sacred for the Maya. It is one of the largest trees in the tropics, measuring up to 70 meters high with roots up to 5 meters deep. No wonder the Maya believed it was the tree of life with its leaves reaching the heavens, its roots connecting to the lower world and its trunk connecting it all to the terrestrial world. As it develops, it is hollow on the inside, enabling humans on earth to ascend or descend after death. While it is hollow, it is less solid so it develops these spikes on its lower portion as a means of protecting it from climbers that can damage its bark.
