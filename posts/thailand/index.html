Bangkok, Thailand

Bangkok, Thailand December, 2019 The Grand Palace The traffic in Bangkok! Seems to take the same amount of time to walk as to drive. Watching the Grab (Uber equivalent) driver’s approach on the app shows them painfully stuck. From the Grand Palace to a modern mall to street food, this is a city of contrasts. But if the traffic and humidity are enough to do you in, then the Thai people are the balance to bring you back. Happy, kind, warm and genuine. The Grand Palace was our starting point today and grand it was! A massive site built in 1782, King Rama must have anticipated the future traffic as he decided to co-locate his residence with the admin offices. Very special in the Palace is the Temple of the Emerald Buddha. The size (66cm tall) belies the stature of this most important Buddha (from the 15th century) in Thailand. He has three seasonal golden costumes and only the King is allowed to change his attire. Right now he’s wearing his full cloak. It’s winter after all. The mosaic and gilt tiles decorating almost everything are stunning! One of the Guardians of the Demons. There are six pair of them, most of which are facing the Emerald Buddha to protect him from evil spirits. The real guardian in this picture is the one with the fancy shoes. Gold certainly brightens up the place! Nobody will try to get past these fearsome creatures. Evan and Alex had to get a new pair of elephant pants for entry into the Grand Palace. While a sarong was good enough for Robyn to cover up, apparently not so for the boys… Dedicated tile fixer or rogue man with a hammer wreaking havoc? Us and Cheewok Komaraphat, considered the father of Thai herbal medicine. He certainly looks like a pretty chill dude…what’s in those herbs? Creative shade at the Siam Centre. There’s a little bit of everything in the traffic here…seemingly going every which way. Previous Next Wat Pho A precious metals day today. We visited Wat Pho, the so called Golden Temple and went to a silver-making workshop. During its construction in 1788, the king moved many Buddhas from abandoned temples and now Golden Buddhas abound here! It is also said to be the first university of Thailand and the birthplace of traditional Thai massage (yes, we did indulge!). While some of these Buddhas are world renowned, the 1,000 other Buddha images are certainly not to be overlooked! The Phra Buddha Saiyas (the reclining Buddha). At 46m long and 15m high, this one takes up an entire building. It is not possible to see the reclining Buddha in its full glory. I feel like I’m being watched… Paint me gold…would I pass as a mini version? Here’s the reclining Buddha if you could see the whole thing at once. The reclining Buddha’s feet are five metres long, inlaid with mother of pearl. They are said to have 108 auspicious symbols in the design. Phra Buddha Theva Patimakorn (the seated Buddha). I made a grave error here. I stretched out one leg and was immediately accosted by a guard. One is never to present one’s feet to the Buddha. Lots of Chinese influence with the ceramic decoration on the Chedi (holding the remains of Buddhas, kings and other important royals. Some of the more modern Chedi have a photo of their occupant incorporated into the tiles. The playful(?) looking guardians sprinkled around the site to guard the pagodas. Originally used as ballasts on the ships exporting rice to China, these ultimately became appreciated as art. Buddha repairs or another rogue person with a putty knife? I’ll let you in on a little secret…all that glitters is not necessarily gold. A Buddha line up. Some of the 400 Buddhas in the cloisters. Resident monks praying. Silver jewellery making at Shannta in the Bangkok arts and cultural centre. Robyn’s on the left, mine on the right. Kazan ramen. The stone bowl is 300F filled with noodles, vegetables and meat. Pour on the soup and cover with what suspiciously looks like a Moroccan tajine lid. Wait a minute and enjoy. Comes with a free facial! Fresh fish? I think I’ll go vegan today. Previous Next Chatuchak Weekend Market It’s Saturday in Bangkok. That means a trip to the Chatuchak Weekend Market, reputed to be the world’s largest weekend market. It was surprisingly quiet…until after lunch. As with most places, it’s best to go early. It’s quite a business with an average of 200,000 visitors per weekend poking around 15,000 stalls selling everything you can imagine including misspelled knock offs and, sadly, illegal wildlife. Capped the day off with a foot massage before dinner. Alex was interviewed by some students for a school project. Perusing the shirts. The ubiquitous elephant pants. Lunch! Best paella ever! A work of art in a pan! Not sure what this instrument is. It’s like a bunch of flutes ties together with a single hole in each. He blows into the dark horizontal piece near the bottom and moves his fingers across the holes just above. A bit of R&R at the rooftop pool. Impressed? Didn’t see any birds on a wire here. No room with all the wires upon wires upon wires. Never seen this before: personal mini keg at your table. Now I’m curious about the durian. It was freeze dried. How bad could it smell? Pretty bad. Well, some cheeses smell bad and are delicious. How bad could this taste? Pretty bad. I had to spit it out and brush my teeth. Don’t bother putting this experience on your bucket list. Previous Next Wat Arun On our last full day in Bangkok we visited Wat Arun, one of the iconic images of Bangkok. On the edge of the Chao Phraya River, this ceramic covered temple is also known as the Temple of Dawn, shining as the sun rises. A little trip on one of the many Chao Phraya River ferries.

Phuket, Thailand

Phuket, Thailand January, 2020 Phang Nga Bay What a way to end 2019! Kayaking in Phang Nga Bay was spectacular! Turquoise waters, tiny mud skippers, cheeky monkeys, tropical birdsong and creating a special offering to the ocean. An hour long journey by boat took us to Phang Nga Bay. We spent the day on and off the boat with our wonderful guides (Soon, M and Mein) from John Gray’s Sea Canoe tour. Don’t miss this day if you come to Phuket. One of the many long tail boats on the Bay. Cool looking crafts but they spew a lot of diesel… Evan in our first hong (“room” in Thai) which is a sea cave. These are part of the collapsed sea caves, open to the sky. They are full of plants and wildlife. Alex and Robyn with the absolutely wonderful Mein. Best guide ever! Adam and Soon navigating around a Mangrove tree. The water in the bay and in the hongs is murky and quite shallow in parts. The bottom is clay. Relaxing in the hong. Rafting up. Better lie down for this tight fit! Bats in the caverns leading to the hongs. Exiting back out to the bay. Long-tailed (or Crab-eating) Macaques live in the trees and scamper along the cave walls. Making our own Krathong with our kayak guides. Such creativity. Krathongs complete and put aside for later. Evan and my Krathong. The base is a section of banana tree trunk decorated with folded banana leaves and flowers. Getting ready to float our krathongs as a forgiveness offering to the goddess of water, floating away our bad luck. Our wonderful guides teamed up and paddled our family kayaks into a triangle for a personal ceremony. To me, our three krathongs represent our three kids who are all embarking on their next life journeys in 2020. Previous Next The Big Buddha The first day of the year filled with adventure and surprises! Sailing, hiking and thievery, oh my! It’s been about 25 years since Adam donned his sailor’s cap but apparently it’s like riding a bike! Taught us our way around a Hobie Cat. …and for some, lounging on the bow deck was just the speed (and splashes) they were after. We drove all the way down island to hike up to Ming Mongkol Buddha (aka The Big Buddha) on the top of Nakkerd Hill. I can telll you we were knackered by the time we got to the top. Think pitch of the Grouse Grind in 32C heat with humidity. A bit of jungle trekking. Made it! And what better way to rehydrate! Best coconut water I’ve ever had. And we peeled off the meat for most of our time at the top…more on that later… The big Buddha is covered in marble tile…mostly. Construction began in 2002 and its still not done. Not quite Sagrada de Familia but it sure is taking a long time. Like the gold leaf for purchase at Wat Arun, here you can buy marble tiles, write a message on the back of it in sharpie and it will be used to tile the floor of the interior. At 45m tall, this Buddha certainly dominates the site. Twin photo bomb. The Naga is the king of snakes and symbolizes protection in Buddhism. You’ll often find it incorporated into the decoration of structures. Here it is a beautiful handrail leading up to the Buddha. So, back to the story of why the coconut meat didn’t last our whole time at the top of Nakkerd Hill. Robyn had been diligently peeling off the meat inside the coconut and took a break, handing it to Adam, while she admired the view over Chalong Bay. Suddenly a monkey appeared out of nowhere and tried to snatch the coconut out of his hands. A tug of war ensued. He was quickly stared down by the fierce, snarling little monkey and was forced to relinquish the coconut. Cute and innocent? Hardly! This is the face of a guilty thief proudly displaying the spoils of his pillaging. Family selfie. Alex is not amused. At least there was no selfie stick involved. Chiang Mai, Thailand

Chiang Mai, Thailand January, 2020 Temples & Tuktuks We are now in Northern Thailand and it feels a bit more like home…except for the heat, the temples, the tuktuks and more motorcycles and mopeds on the roads than cars. Ok. Let’s just say it’s a lot greener up here. Chiang Mai is 730 years old and the city boasts 300 temples (36 of which are in the very small old town). There are still remnants of the old city walls, aided in its day by a moat in keeping out the ancient intruders. Looks like they need to start a new gold leaf campaign. Some are peeling. I guess not everyone gets the joke… Following a Sagrada de Familia timeframe, construction of Wat Chedi Luang dates back to the 14th century when the then king wanted a place to bury his father’s ashes. Construction slowed and it took until the mid-15th century until it was complete. This was the original home of the most revered emerald Buddha which we saw in the Grand Palace in Bangkok. A hundred years later, it lost 30m off its top in an earthquake and then suffered further damage in 1775 when the city was retaken from the Burmese. But here it is, still standing proudly! One of four stairs guarded by Naga snakes at Wat Chedi Luang. Finally, a decent Hokkaido brown sugar milk tea! The market at Wat Phra Singh, one of the most popular temples in Chiang Mai. Fascinating group of elderly meditating monks in the main viharn (assembly hall) at Wat Phra Singh. It’s quite mind-blowing when you get close enough to realize that they are wax models! Astonishing detail down to the nose and ear hairs! Madame Tussaud could get a tip or two from here! One of the meditating monks. His expression is prescient of the future smart phone holders snapping pictures! Bells abound in Thai temples. The ringing of the bells has a religious significance offering protection from evil spirits and purifying the holy place. It gets a little much when kids are running around ringing bell after bell. There was quite a controversy in Bangkok a few years ago when expats living near a temple tried to get the police to get the monks to stop ringing the bells. Add a bit of gold leaf and you wouldn’t be able to tell these two apart. Our first ride in a tuk tuk! Not enough room for Alex so he was hanging out the side. I got to ride shotgun! Dinner at the night market. A bargain for the scorpions on a stick but it just wasn’t enough to entice me. Previous Next Doi Suthep Today’s hike up Doi Suthep along the Monk’s Trail was through another tropical forest and carried us to another time. We came out of the forest into the most amazing temple grounds of Wat Pha Lat. It felt like a well-hidden sacred place that only a few have the luck of discovering. A much steeper trail then continued on to Wat Phra That. This temple at the top of the mountain is much more touristy. The “easy” part of the hike from the base to the first temple. Heading up to the temple between fierce-looking lions. The one on the left has just his tongue in his mouth but zoom in and check what’s in the mouth of the one on the right! Some of the statues are adorned with jewels and many have creative offerings left by locals. Some of the statues are adorned with jewels and many have creative offerings left by locals. Interesting offerings for this jolly fellow…a pack of cigarettes wrapped in a banana leaf and a bottle of water. A more classic offering of flowers set against the crumbling brick base of the stupa. Siblings! A beautiful door to what feels like a creepy entrance. I’m not sure where this one leads…. Stunning view at Wat Pha Lat. Lots of Buddhist inspirational messages on trees all over Wat Pha Lat. Words to live by that would make the world a better place. Joy. Wat Pha Lat is full of what seem like ancient statues and Buddha shrines. Much is outdoors, nestled among bamboo, palms, trees and rock pools fed by a small waterfall. At the top! The view over Chiang Mai from the top of the mountain. Lots of colour at Wat Phra That, in comparison to the ancient-looking natural stone at Wat Pha Lat. The songthaew stand at the top of Doi Suthep. This is the original taxi in Thailand. They are basically a share taxi for people heading in the same direction. Two benches face each other and the driver waits to be full before he departs. Our first ride in a songthaew from the top of Doi Suthap back to Old Chiang Mai. We ended up being about 14 people stuffed in here…we were the only hikers, thankfully dried off by then! Beautiful street signs in Chiang Mai’s Old City. Light and extra light. Mae Rim, Thailand

Mae Rim, Thailand January, 2020 Elephant Sanctuary Today was up close and personal with the most majestic of creatures followed by the impossible feat of climbing a waterfall freehand…very much a Spiderman moment! All dimples and smiles! These enormous yet gentle animals are truly incredible. We had the pleasure of spending a morning with them at Mae Rim, feeding them sugar cane and bananas (they eat 10% of their body weight every day), mud bathing with them, and washing them with home made shampoo (we pounded the root of a plant that then became sudsy in water). We had a lot of discussions amongst ourselves as to the ethics behind these elephant “sanctuaries”. The jury is still out but with a lifespan of up to 100 years, those freed from servitude need a place to live out their days in peace. We dressed in the traditional garb of the mahout. The mahout is the elephant keeper who bonds with the elephant for life. When these elephants are rescued, the mahout will live with the elephant at the sanctuary. Feeding each other. New elephant tail hairdo for Alex. Mom, look what I found. Can I keep her? An elephant hug is way better than a bear hug. Just waiting for the elephant to pick Evan up. That’s Adam and Alex on the left mudding up the elephant before the mudslinging got a little more personal. The calm before the storm… Robyn and me giving the elephant a mud bath. Our guide, Mr. Tony, wrote our names in Thai on our arms. We thought it was a cool way to show us what our names looked like phonetically. Then we realized it was really his way of remembering who was who! Our wonderful guide, Goi, is an avid outdoors enthusiast took us not only to the Elephant Sanctuary but also to Bua Tong. His dream destination is Whistler to go mountain biking and snowboarding (he now knows that means two visits)-. Here’s how we met: he was our taxi driver from the airport. Chatty people that we all are, it seemed like a match made in heaven and we exchanged numbers. He has excellent English and does bike tours in the region when he’s not driving his very nice SUV taxi. Look Ma, no hands! Affectionately known as Sticky Falls, limestone deposits coat the rocks making them feel “sticky”, a little like sandpaper. Where else could one walk up waterfalls against the rushing of water? Surfing. The white parts of the rocks are grippy while the greener it gets, the more slippery. Goi shared a little secret. The green is the algal build up that happens throughout the day over the whole surface. Early in the morning before the place opens up to the public, the staff are out there scrubbing off the algae of the previous day. Refreshing, that’s for sure! A zen moment to end the day. Samoeng Tai, Thailand

Samoeng Tai, Thailand January, 2020 Karen Hill Tribe An early start to the day to visit a Karen Hill Tribe community two hours deep into the mountains. En route through a Hmong village. Here they are growing garlic. In an effort to stop the booming opium business in Thailand, the previous King (Rama 9) went to visit the hill tribes who were growing poppies and helped them shift to less nefarious crops. The peaceful hill tribes were happy to oblige when presented not only with an explanation of the problem but with a doable and practical solution. A classic example of respectful negotiations. A rose farm run by refugees from Myanmar. Rows and rows of different coloured blooms kept intact and free of insects in their white foam covers. One of the rose farmer’s kids. She was stunningly sweet with an infectious laugh. Her older brother was a bit of a monkey, climbing up the support beams of the house and jumping the 8 or 10 feet down to the bamboo deck. The baby sat there crying perhaps shocked by the unusual looking people at his home. Not sure where the parents were… Which came first? A stop at the local market to buy ingredients for lunch. Our lunch chef getting the wok fired up. She cooks in the covered part of the back of the house. This is also where the family sleeps. Eating and lounging is in the front of the house. What an amazing lunch! The dining room. Our host family for lunch. The most beautiful matriarch of our host family. In the Karen tradition, unmarried girls wear a one piece white dress. Once married, women no longer wear white and they move to skirts and tops. You can distinguish the different tribes by their clothing. Each tribe has a particular pattern to their weaving. We met a lovely young woman (married as denoted by her clothing)weaving the cloth that she will then make into sarongs and bags destined for sale at the markets and shops in town. Now that harvest season is over and planting season is a few months away, she has downtime enabling her to focus on the weaving. We bought this piece of fabric before she finished it into a skirt. It will her a week to weave this fabric. As a means of supporting entrepreneurship and small business in these villages, the government built roads, set up local markets and created distribution. This village got a paved road three years ago…and electricity one year ago. Some of the villagers grow coffee. This is the inside of the coffee cherry before it is picked, dried and sold to a toaster. The flesh actually tastes sweet! How to make friends with a buffalo? Feed it salt water. These buffalo are no longer field work animals. They are kept for investment (if the village runs short of cash, they can sell one) and for fertilizer. After the rice harvest, the paddies are planted with grass for the buffalo to graze…and fertilize in their natural way. While rice uses quite a lot of water to grow, it doesn’t pull a tone of nutrients from the soil. As a result, combined with all the buffalo fertilizer, these fields not not need to be left fallow to regenerate their nutrients. Heading off through the rice fields on a jungle hike with our guide Chai who is of the Karen tribe (but from a different village on the other side of Mount Inthanon, which is part of the Himalayan mountain range). The Karen people originally came from Mongolia, through China and then Myanmar before ending up in Thailand. There are some 400,000 Karen people in Thailand with another seven million who remained in Myanmar. During our jungle hike, we came across this offering to the jungle gods. This was a villagers way of asking for forgiveness before setting out on a hunt. Neighbours cooking sticky rice in a special type of bamboo. They stuff the rice into the bamboo and soak overnight on coconut water. Then it is cooked over a fire before peeling the outside layers off to eat and enjoy! Very tasty.
